Arnold Strassberg held 51 percent of Martin Ice Cream Company, a wholesale ice cream distributor in Bloomfield. He had spent years building the business of selling super-premium ice cream into supermarkets, on the strength of relationships with supermarket owners that predated the company and a handshake understanding with the founder of Häagen-Dazs. The company had the benefit of all of it.

When Häagen-Dazs paid $1,430,340 for the supermarket distribution rights, the Commissioner took the position that the corporation was the real seller and owed corporate-level tax on the gain.

The Tax Court said the corporation had never owned the assets. Strassberg "never entered into a covenant not to compete with petitioner or any other agreement — not even an employment agreement — by which any of [his] distribution agreements ..., relationships with the supermarkets, and ... distribution expertise became the property of petitioner." Personal relationships of a shareholder-employee are not corporate assets when the employee has no contract with the corporation, and they are "entirely distinct from the intangible corporate asset of corporate goodwill." *Martin Ice Cream Co. v. Commissioner*, 110 T.C. 189, 207 (1998).

The company had run for a decade and a half on assets it did not own. Nobody noticed until someone tried to sell them.

That is the fact pattern in most closely held businesses, and it becomes a valuation event the day one owner leaves.

## The covenant is not paperwork. It is the conveyance.

Goodwill is reputation likely to generate future business. New Jersey has said so since [*Dugan v. Dugan*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/dugan-v-dugan-92-nj-423/), 92 N.J. 423 (1983), and the same opinion drew the line that decides these cases: the portion with value is the portion that exists as a going concern independent of the individual — value a successor could buy and keep. Absent an enforceable covenant, the practitioner takes the rest with him.

That sentence is usually read as a rule about divorce. It is a rule about ownership, and it applies with equal force when the buyer is a partner, a private equity fund, or the company itself.

The mechanics are worth stating precisely, because two very different arguments get run together.

A key-person discount says the entity owns an asset whose value depends on someone who may leave, and reduces the value of that asset. It is a going-concern adjustment taken against enterprise value, before any shareholder-level discount, and its size is a question of evidence rather than label. [*Estate of Mitchell v. Commissioner*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/estate-of-mitchell-v-commissioner-250-f3d-696/), 250 F.3d 696 (9th Cir. 2001). It also invites the obvious response: the person is not actually leaving.

A personal goodwill argument says the value was never the entity's to begin with. There is nothing to discount, because there is nothing there. That is *Martin Ice Cream*, and it is a far larger adjustment than any percentage a key-person analysis will produce.

Which argument is available depends almost entirely on whether anyone signed a covenant. This is why two competent appraisers who agree on every multiple, every rate, and every normalization adjustment can still be far apart in a single-rainmaker company. They are not disagreeing about arithmetic. One of them thinks the client relationships are institutional, and the other thinks they are personal, and no amount of modeling resolves that.

## New Jersey enforces two different covenants, and the difference is worth money

Here is the part that changes what counsel should actually do, and it is nearly fifty-five years old.

New Jersey does not treat all restrictive covenants alike. In *Solari Industries, Inc. v. Malady*, 55 N.J. 571 (1970), the Supreme Court adopted the rule that a noncompetitive agreement may receive total or partial enforcement to the extent reasonable under the circumstances — abandoning the all-or-nothing approach — and it drew a distinction that practitioners routinely miss. A seller's covenant "designed to protect the good will of the business for the buyer is freely enforceable." An employee's covenant not to compete after termination "is not as freely enforceable because of well recognized countervailing policy considerations." 55 N.J. at 576.

An employee covenant is tested against three requirements. It must simply protect the legitimate interests of the employer, impose no undue hardship on the employee, and not be injurious to the public. Same page.

*Whitmyer Bros., Inc. v. Doyle*, 58 N.J. 25 (1971), then defined the first requirement, and defined it narrowly. "The employer has no legitimate interest in preventing competition as such." What the employer may protect is trade secrets, confidential business information, and customer relationships. Matters of general knowledge within the industry are not trade secrets, and "routine or trivial differences in practices and methods" will not support a restraint. 58 N.J. at 33-34.

The public-interest prong is not decorative either. The Supreme Court applied the *Solari* test to a physician's covenant in *Community Hospital Group, Inc. v. More*, 183 N.J. 36 (2005), weighing the public's interest in access to care against the employer's interest in enforcement, and modified the restraint rather than enforcing or voiding it whole.

So the planning consequence is direct. A covenant signed at a buyout closing, in consideration for the purchase price and as part of the sale of the departing owner's interest and goodwill, is a seller's covenant, and it is the enforceable kind. A covenant a controlling owner tries to impose on a co-owner-employee in year eight, for no new consideration, is the other kind, and it will be measured against *Whitmyer*'s narrow list of protectable interests.

The moment of maximum leverage to obtain the covenant is the same moment the covenant is most enforceable. That is not a coincidence, and it is the single most useful thing in this analysis.

## What the missing covenant does to the number in a live buyout

Set the scene that actually arises. Two owners. One is bought out under the operating agreement or under [N.J.S.A. 14A:12-7](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/njsa-14a-12-7/)(8). The continuing owner is the buyer and will fund the price. The departing owner is free to open across the street on Monday.

Three things happen to the valuation.

The transferable goodwill shrinks. Whatever share of the earnings depends on relationships the departing owner can take is not enterprise value in any durable sense. The measurement runs through the excess-earnings method [*Levy v. Levy*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/levy-v-levy-164-nj-super-542/), 164 N.J. Super. 542 (Ch. Div. 1978), supplied and [*Dugan*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/dugan-v-dugan-92-nj-423/) adopted: what the owner earns above what a comparably credentialed employee would be paid, capitalized over the years a purchaser would pay for in advance. Reasonable compensation is doing the work, and it is where these fights are won.

The risk premium rises. Even where the goodwill is institutional, the buyer is acquiring earnings exposed to an identified competitor with perfect knowledge of the customer base, the pricing, and the weaknesses. That is a real increase in the required rate of return, and it is separate from the goodwill question.

And the standard of value decides how much of any of this the buyer actually captures. In a statutory fair-value buyout the court values the enterprise as a going concern and awards a proportionate share, and in New Jersey the discount question is equitable and fact-specific rather than categorical. Compare [*Balsamides v. Protameen Chemicals, Inc.*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/balsamides-v-protameen-160-nj-352/), 160 N.J. 352 (1999), sustaining a marketability discount where the oppressor was the seller, with [*Lawson Mardon Wheaton, Inc. v. Smith*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/lawson-mardon-wheaton-v-smith-160-nj-383/), 160 N.J. 383 (1999), holding such a discount ordinarily unavailable to dissenters absent extraordinary circumstances.

## The doctrine that cuts the other way

The departing owner is not as free as the buyer's expert will assume, and the argument that he is should be pressed hard before it is priced.

Trade secrets and confidential information are protected without any covenant at all. *Whitmyer* said so expressly, citing *Sun Dial Corp. v. Rideout*, 16 N.J. 252 (1954), and noted that a reasonably confined covenant serves mainly to avoid difficulties of definition and proof. A customer list assembled at the company's expense, pricing files, and formulations do not walk out the door merely because nobody signed anything.

While he remains an owner, he owes duties. A member or shareholder of a closely held New Jersey entity who diverts a corporate opportunity, solicits employees, or competes during his tenure is not exercising a freedom; he is generating a claim. And the claim is worth more than a covenant would have been, because the remedy is not limited to an injunction.

Conduct also prices the outcome directly. In [*Wisniewski v. Walsh*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/wisniewski-v-walsh-2015-nj-super-unpub-lexis-3001/), 2015 N.J. Super. Unpub. LEXIS 3001 (App. Div. Dec. 24, 2015), a marketability discount was sustained against the selling shareholder whose own conduct had driven the litigation. Nothing about the marketability of a trucking company turns on how a shareholder behaved. The discount was doing equitable work under an economic label — which is a fair criticism of the reasoning and beside the point for planning. The record on who did what is a valuation record.

Then the argument that defeats the whole discount, and it is the one to expect. The cure is available. If the departing owner will sign a covenant as part of the buyout, the risk being priced disappears, and a buyer who declines to negotiate for one has chosen the discount over the protection. The response is to make the covenant a term of the deal rather than an argument about the appraisal. Price the interest with the covenant, and condition the price on it.

Finally, the covenant may not be worth what the model assumes. A five-year nationwide restraint on a departing minority owner with no trade secrets and no customer contact is unlikely to survive *Whitmyer*, and a court applying *Solari* will enforce it only to the extent reasonable. Valuing a company as though an overbroad covenant were fully effective overstates the number in exactly the way the absence of one understates it.

## What to do about it

In a live dispute, negotiate the covenant before arguing about the discount. The two are substitutes. Whichever side holds the ability to compete holds the leverage, and the sequence that produces the best outcome for the buyer is to buy the protection rather than litigate the percentage.

Make it a seller's covenant on the face of the document. Recite that it is given in connection with the sale of the interest and the goodwill associated with it, and allocate consideration to it. *Solari* treats that covenant as freely enforceable; the employment-style covenant is the one that gets scrutinized.

Scope it to a protectable interest. *Whitmyer* permits protection of trade secrets, confidential business information, and customer relationships, and nothing else. The durable restraint is the one *Solari* itself suggested on remand: a limited restriction on dealing with actual and prospective customers with whom the departing owner had substantial dealings, for a defined period.

Get non-solicitation of employees and customers separately, and treat those as the core rather than as add-ons. They protect what is actually at risk, and they impose far less hardship, which makes them far more likely to be enforced as written.

In planning, sign them at formation, and re-sign them at every event that supplies consideration — an admission, a promotion, an equity grant, an amendment to the operating agreement. Consideration is where these agreements fail most often in closely held companies, and each of those events is a chance to fix it.

And instruct the appraiser explicitly. Whether the valuation assumes a covenant, and whose covenant, is an engagement assumption that changes the conclusion by more than any input in the model. An appraisal that is silent on it has answered a question nobody asked.

The covenant nobody wanted to raise at formation is the cheapest asset the company will ever acquire. It is cheap precisely because it must be bought before anyone is thinking about leaving, and it is expensive for the same reason.

## Frequently asked questions

**We are equal owners and nobody signed a non-compete. Can my partner compete if I buy him out?** Absent a covenant, generally yes once he is no longer an owner, subject to trade secret and confidential information protections that exist independently. *Whitmyer Bros., Inc. v. Doyle*, 58 N.J. 25, 33-34 (1971), holds that an employer has no legitimate interest in preventing competition as such and may protect only trade secrets, confidential business information, and customer relationships. Negotiate the covenant as a term of the buyout; a covenant given in connection with the sale of an interest is the freely enforceable kind under *Solari Industries, Inc. v. Malady*, 55 N.J. 571, 576 (1970).

**Does the missing covenant reduce what I have to pay for his shares?** It should, and the mechanism matters. If the goodwill is personal to him, it may not be enterprise value at all — the reasoning of *Martin Ice Cream Co. v. Commissioner*, 110 T.C. 189 (1998), and of [*Dugan v. Dugan*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/dugan-v-dugan-92-nj-423/), 92 N.J. 423 (1983). If the goodwill is institutional but he is irreplaceable, it is a key-person adjustment against enterprise value before any shareholder-level discount. [*Estate of Mitchell v. Commissioner*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/estate-of-mitchell-v-commissioner-250-f3d-696/), 250 F.3d 696 (9th Cir. 2001). Expect the other side to argue that you could have bought a covenant and chose not to.

**Can I make my co-owner sign a non-compete now?** You can ask, and the answer depends on consideration and on what you are protecting. A covenant imposed mid-relationship on an owner-employee is measured as an employee covenant under the three-part *Solari* test — legitimate interest, no undue hardship, not injurious to the public — rather than as a seller's covenant. Tie it to an event that supplies real consideration, such as an equity grant, an admission, or an amendment to the operating agreement.

**Is a non-compete enforceable in New Jersey at all?** Yes, within limits, and courts will trim rather than void. *Solari* adopted total or partial enforcement to the extent reasonable under the circumstances, and *Community Hospital Group, Inc. v. More*, 183 N.J. 36 (2005), applied the test to a physician's covenant and modified the restraint after weighing the public interest in access to care. An overbroad restraint is therefore not fatal, but it is also not worth what a valuation assuming full enforcement implies.

**What is this actually costing me as an owner?** In a company where a meaningful share of revenue follows one person, the difference between goodwill treated as institutional and goodwill treated as personal routinely exceeds every other disputed item combined, because it is not a percentage adjustment to the number — it removes an asset from it. Reasonable compensation is the hinge: the excess-earnings method of [*Levy v. Levy*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/levy-v-levy-164-nj-super-542/), 164 N.J. Super. 542 (Ch. Div. 1978), capitalizes only what the owner earns above a comparable employee's pay, so moving that figure moves the goodwill by a multiple of the change.
