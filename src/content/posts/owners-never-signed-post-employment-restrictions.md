Goodwill is what a buyer pays for above the value of the hard assets. In a closely held company, much of that goodwill is personal — it lives in the owners' relationships, reputations, and know-how. Restrictive covenants are what convert personal goodwill into enterprise goodwill the buyer can actually own. Without them, the conversion is incomplete.

In a buyout, the continuing owner is the buyer, and the departing owner is free to compete. That freedom is not academic. It is a live risk that the customers, referral sources, and employees the business depends on will follow the departing owner out. A rational buyer prices that risk, and the price is a reduction in value.

This is where the absence of planning becomes a valuation event. Had the covenants been signed at formation, the goodwill would transfer cleanly. Because they were not, the appraiser confronts a genuine question about how much of the enterprise's value is durable in the departing owner's absence.

The lesson runs in two directions. In a live dispute, the party positioned to compete holds leverage the valuation must account for. In planning, covenants signed at the beginning — when everyone is friendly and no one is leaving — are among the cheapest value protection an owner will ever buy.
