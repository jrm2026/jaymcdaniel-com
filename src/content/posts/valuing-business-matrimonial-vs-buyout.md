A New Jersey trial court had to value a 47½ percent interest in a family wholesale florist business and divide it between a husband and wife. The husband's appraiser did what appraisers ordinarily do with a minority block of a private company. He valued the enterprise, took the proportionate share, and then reduced it twice — once because the block could not control the company, and again because no one could readily sell it.

Both adjustments were conventional. Together they removed a large part of the number.

The trial court took them back out, and the Appellate Division affirmed. Neither a minority nor a marketability discount applies to a closely held interest valued for equitable distribution, the panel held, reasoning from two oppressed-shareholder buyout decisions handed down three years earlier and from the American Law Institute's Principles of Corporate Governance § 7.22(a). [*Brown v. Brown*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/brown-v-brown-348-nj-super-466/), 348 N.J. Super. 466 (App. Div. 2002).

The appraisal was not incompetent. It answered a question the case had not asked.

That is the entire problem, and it recurs every time a business shows up in a matrimonial docket. Value is not a fact about a company. It is an answer, and the legal question sets the standard of value that produces it. Change the question and the same company on the same day is worth something materially different.

## The discount rule reaches matrimonial court through a different door

[*Balsamides v. Protameen Chemicals, Inc.*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/balsamides-v-protameen-160-nj-352/), 160 N.J. 352 (1999), and [*Lawson Mardon Wheaton, Inc. v. Smith*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/lawson-mardon-wheaton-v-smith-160-nj-383/), 160 N.J. 383 (1999), are corporate cases. Neither involved a marriage. They were decided the same day and they addressed statutory fair value — one in an oppression buyout, the other in a dissenters' rights appraisal.

*Brown* imported their rationale anyway, and the reason it transfers is structural rather than doctrinal. In an equitable distribution the interest is not sold. It is counted, assigned to the titled spouse, and offset against other marital assets. There is no buyer, no transfer, and no transaction of any kind.

A marketability discount prices the difficulty of selling. Where nothing is being sold, the discount prices an event that is not happening.

Notice what follows, because practitioners usually have it backwards. The matrimonial rule on discounts is more categorical than New Jersey's buyout rule, not less. *Lawson Mardon* held that a marketability discount ordinarily should not be applied to dissenting shareholders' stock absent extraordinary circumstances. *Balsamides*, decided the same day, upheld one — because the oppressing owner was the seller and the oppressed owner was the buyer, and an undiscounted price would have rewarded the party whose conduct caused the remedy. New Jersey fair value is equitable and fact-sensitive on discounts. The equitable-distribution number, after *Brown*, is not.

## Three questions, three numbers

Three standards of value can be applied to one company. They are not interchangeable, and no appraisal is competent until someone has chosen among them.

Fair market value asks what a hypothetical willing buyer would pay a hypothetical willing seller, neither compelled, both reasonably informed. [Revenue Ruling 59-60](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/revenue-ruling-59-60/), 1959-1 C.B. 237. It contemplates an actual arm's-length exchange of the specific interest, so illiquidity and lack of control are real costs a real buyer would price. This is the standard for estate and gift tax, and it is the one most closely held appraisals are built to.

Statutory fair value asks what the owner's proportionate share of the enterprise is worth as a going concern. Delaware fixed that unit of valuation in [*Cavalier Oil Corp. v. Harnett*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/cavalier-oil-v-harnett-564-a2d-1137/), 564 A.2d 1137 (Del. 1989), and New Jersey runs the same way under [N.J.S.A. 14A:12-7](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/njsa-14a-12-7/)(8) for oppression buyouts and [N.J.S.A. 14A:11-1 to -11](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/njsa-14a-11-1-to-11/) for dissenters. Here there is a transfer, and the equities of who is buying from whom are in play.

Equitable-distribution value asks what the marital estate holds. There is no transfer at all, and the discount question is answered before it is asked.

The gap between the first and the third is where matrimonial cases are won and lost. On a middle-market company, a combined minority and marketability reduction routinely runs from a quarter to nearly half of the pro-rata figure. That is not a rounding difference between experts. It is often larger than everything else in the case.

## Where the matrimonial number runs higher than the buyout number

Two features push it up.

The discounts come out. A fair-market-value appraisal prepared for a gift, a sale, or an estate return carries them as a matter of course, and a matrimonial lawyer who accepts that report as delivered has conceded the largest single adjustment in the case without argument. *Brown* is the authority that they do not belong there.

And goodwill comes in. New Jersey has counted the goodwill of a professional practice as distributable property since [*Dugan v. Dugan*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/dugan-v-dugan-92-nj-423/), 92 N.J. 423 (1983), which defined goodwill as reputation likely to generate future business and made the distributable portion the value that exists as a going concern independent of the practitioner. [*Piscopo v. Piscopo*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/piscopo-v-piscopo-232-nj-super-559/), 232 N.J. Super. 559 (App. Div. 1989), carried the principle past the professional office to a performer's reputation and held that difficulty of valuation does not affect includability. The measurement runs through the excess-earnings method [*Levy v. Levy*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/levy-v-levy-164-nj-super-542/), 164 N.J. Super. 542 (Ch. Div. 1978), supplied: what the owner earns above what a comparably credentialed employee would be paid, capitalized over the years a purchaser would pay for in advance.

Then the consequence nobody enjoys explaining to a client. The titled spouse pays cash for a share of an asset that produced no cash and may never be sold. *Dugan* named that problem in 1983 and left it open.

## Where it runs lower

Three doctrines cut the other way, and counsel for the titled spouse who does not raise all three has left money on the table.

The first is whether this owner has goodwill at all. [*Slutsky v. Slutsky*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/slutsky-v-slutsky-451-nj-super-332/), 451 N.J. Super. 332 (App. Div. 2017), is the case most often cited for the wrong proposition. The husband's expert never said the law firm lacked goodwill. He said this partner — a non-originating partner paid the market rate for his work — had none beyond his compensation, because there were no excess earnings to capitalize. A finding that the enterprise enjoys reputation establishes nothing about that. Firm goodwill is not partner goodwill, and after *Slutsky*, imputing the one to the other is reversible error rather than a weak inference.

The second is whether the goodwill is the spouse's to keep. In [*Seiler v. Seiler*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/seiler-v-seiler-308-nj-super-474/), 308 N.J. Super. 474 (App. Div. 1998), the husband ran an exclusive Allstate agency. Allstate owned the equipment, assigned the telephone number, designed the advertising, and controlled hiring. The agency had goodwill. It belonged to Allstate. Nothing was distributable in the agent's hands. Franchisees on a tight leash, captive producers, and anyone whose book of business reverts to the firm on departure raise the same threshold question, and it comes before any valuation method.

The third is the size of the share. *Slutsky* invoked the catch-all equitable-distribution factor, [N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23.1](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/njsa-2a-34-23-1/)(p), to direct that "a measure of consideration must be given to the lack of intrinsic value associated with any amount determined as individual goodwill." That is statutory authority to award the non-titled spouse less than half of an asset the titled spouse can never sell and must fund out of pocket. Equitable is not synonymous with equal, and the statute requires specific findings on asset valuation and distribution alike.

There is a fourth, and it is procedural. [*Steneken v. Steneken*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/steneken-v-steneken-183-nj-290/), 183 N.J. 290 (2005), held that valuing a business on normalized owner compensation while setting alimony from actual income is not impermissible double counting — actual income remains the lodestar for alimony. But the Court subjected the interplay between alimony and equitable distribution to an overarching concept of fairness. The same excess earnings that were capitalized into the goodwill figure will fund the alimony for years. Nothing forbids using both. Everything requires the court to consider the combined burden, and that consideration has to be requested. Counsel who does not put it on the record has waived the most valuable adjustment available.

## The shareholder agreement may decide both cases

Before either side commissions an appraisal, someone should read the operating or shareholder agreement, because it may moot the exercise.

[*Stern v. Stern*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/stern-v-stern-66-nj-340/), 66 N.J. 340 (1975), attached a presumption to it. Where a partnership or shareholder agreement fixes the value of an interest, and it is established that the firm's books are well kept and the partners' interests are in fact periodically and carefully reviewed, that valuation is subject to effective attack only on clear and convincing proof. Not a preponderance. *Slutsky* reaffirmed the presumption and directed the trial court to test the objectiveness and accuracy of the firm's formula.

The corollary is the part that moves money. An agreement whose values are maintained on a schedule is close to a presumption of value. One signed years ago and never reopened is considerably more vulnerable. In the corporate setting [*Namerow v. PediatriCare Associates, LLC*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/namerow-v-pediatricare-461-nj-super-133/), 461 N.J. Super. 133 (Ch. Div. 2018), enforced a stale certificate of agreed value against a retiring physician despite sixteen years of contrary practice, because the contract was unambiguous and the court would not go outside its four corners.

So the same document can set the buyout price and anchor the divorce number, and it usually does so without anyone having planned it that way.

## The doctrine that cuts the other way

State the counterargument at its strongest, because it is not frivolous.

The titled spouse keeps an interest he did not buy and cannot freely sell, and the day he does sell it — under the buy-sell, to a partner, to a third party — he will receive a discounted price, because that is what a real transaction produces. Refusing the discount in the divorce therefore charges him for value he will never realize, and it charges him in cash today.

That argument has force, and it has an answer. *Brown* does not say the discount is economically unreal. It says the discount does not belong in this computation, because equitable distribution is not a sale. Where the titled spouse's illiquidity is genuine, the remedy is the one *Slutsky* located in N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23.1(p) — a smaller share, supported by findings — together with the payment terms, not a haircut buried in the appraisal.

Two further limits are worth stating plainly. *Brown* is an Appellate Division decision, and the panel remanded on the equitable-distribution, counsel-fee, and expert-fee questions; the discount holding is the part that travels. And on the buyout side, the rule was never absolute to begin with: *Lawson Mardon* said "ordinarily" and "absent extraordinary circumstances," and *Balsamides* is what an extraordinary circumstance looks like.

## Where these reports break

Read the engagement letter first. An appraisal will state the standard of value, and one drafted for an estate return, a bank, or a buy-sell will state fair market value. That single line determines whether the discounts are in the number, and it is settled before the appraiser opens a single financial statement.

Ask what the report was originally prepared for. Recycled valuations are common in matrimonial practice — the gift-tax appraisal from the family transfer, the bank's valuation for a credit facility, the buy-sell certificate. Each was built to answer a different question, and each carries the assumptions of the question it was built for.

Confirm that goodwill has been located before it is measured. *Seiler* is the threshold, *Slutsky* is the allocation, and an expert who capitalized firm-level excess earnings and attributed them to a non-originating partner has skipped both.

Check the reasonable-compensation figure, because it is doing more work than any other input. The excess-earnings method subtracts what a comparable employee would earn from what the owner actually earns. Move that subtrahend by twenty percent and the goodwill number moves by far more. It is also the same figure *Steneken* puts in play on the alimony side.

Reconcile the valuation date against what actually happened in the business. A number computed as of the complaint and tried three years later has absorbed three years of results nobody has accounted for.

And model the payment. A distributive award funded over time, secured or unsecured, at an interest rate the court sets, is a different economic outcome from the same number paid at closing. A spouse who wins on value and loses on terms holds an unsecured receivable from the person across the table.

The company does not change when the caption does. The question does, and the number follows the question.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do minority and marketability discounts apply when valuing a business in a New Jersey divorce?** No. [*Brown v. Brown*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/brown-v-brown-348-nj-super-466/), 348 N.J. Super. 466 (App. Div. 2002), held that neither discount applies to a closely held interest valued for equitable distribution, applying the rationale of [*Balsamides v. Protameen Chemicals, Inc.*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/balsamides-v-protameen-160-nj-352/), 160 N.J. 352 (1999), and [*Lawson Mardon Wheaton, Inc. v. Smith*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/lawson-mardon-wheaton-v-smith-160-nj-383/), 160 N.J. 383 (1999). The reason is that the interest is counted and offset rather than sold, so a discount for illiquidity prices a transaction that is not occurring.

**Can I use my estate-planning appraisal in the divorce?** Only as a starting point, and only after the discounts are removed. An estate or gift appraisal is prepared to the fair-market-value standard of [Revenue Ruling 59-60](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/revenue-ruling-59-60/), which contemplates a hypothetical willing buyer and therefore prices lack of control and lack of marketability. Those adjustments are appropriate to that question and impermissible under *Brown* in equitable distribution.

**Why would the divorce number exceed what a partner would pay me for the same interest?** Because a buyout is a real transaction and a divorce is an accounting. A statutory fair-value buyout awards a proportionate share of the going concern, [*Cavalier Oil Corp. v. Harnett*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/cavalier-oil-v-harnett-564-a2d-1137/), 564 A.2d 1137 (Del. 1989), and in New Jersey the equities of who buys from whom can support a marketability discount, as *Balsamides* did. A negotiated buy-sell price is lower still, because it is constrained by what the buyer can actually fund.

**I will have to pay cash for goodwill I cannot sell. Is there any relief?** Yes, and it must be requested. Under [N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23.1](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/njsa-2a-34-23-1/)(p), [*Slutsky v. Slutsky*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/slutsky-v-slutsky-451-nj-super-332/), 451 N.J. Super. 332 (App. Div. 2017), directed that consideration be given to the lack of intrinsic value associated with individual goodwill, which supports awarding the non-titled spouse less than half. [*Steneken v. Steneken*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/steneken-v-steneken-183-nj-290/), 183 N.J. 290 (2005), separately requires the court to weigh the combined fairness of the alimony and equitable-distribution results.

**Does our shareholder agreement control the value in the divorce?** Often, and more strongly than counsel expects. [*Stern v. Stern*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/stern-v-stern-66-nj-340/), 66 N.J. 340 (1975), holds that where an agreement fixes the value of an interest and the firm's books are well kept and interests periodically and carefully reviewed, the valuation may be displaced only by clear and convincing proof. The presumption protects a maintained agreement; a formula last updated years ago and never revisited is far more vulnerable.
