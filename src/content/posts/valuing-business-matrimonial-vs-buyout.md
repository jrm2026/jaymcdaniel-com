Value is not a single fact about a business. It is an answer to a question, and the question is set by the legal context. Change the standard of value — fair value, fair market value, value to the holder — and you change the number, sometimes dramatically, for the very same company on the very same date.

Matrimonial practice adds its own wrinkles. Many jurisdictions distinguish enterprise goodwill, which is a marital asset, from personal goodwill, which in some states is not. Marketability and minority discounts may be treated differently than they are in a commercial buyout. And the valuation date, the treatment of the owner-spouse's compensation, and the standard the state applies all move the result.

The practical risk is importing the wrong framework. A matrimonial lawyer who accepts a fair-market-value appraisal built for a sale, in a state that applies a value-to-the-holder standard without marketability discounts, may be working from a number that understates the marital estate by a wide margin.

The discipline is to fix the standard first, then value to it. Get the legal question right, and the appraisal follows. Get it wrong, and even a technically competent appraisal answers a question the case never asked.
