The move is to surface that buyer before the valuation date, not after — and the record has to show it. A strategic buyer values the company for what it adds to something the buyer already owns: a distribution channel, a customer list, a capability that closes a gap. That buyer will pay more than a financial buyer, and more than the operating owner ever booked, because the synergies are real to the buyer even if they never showed up on the seller's income statement.

In a dissolution, the minority owner's leverage is that fair value contemplates the enterprise as a going concern in the hands of a willing buyer. If a strategic buyer exists — or can be shown to exist — the going-concern number is the strategic number, not the lifestyle-business number the majority would prefer the court to adopt.

The tactical error is to raise the strategic buyer after the valuation date, when it reads as litigation-driven invention. Do the opposite. Build the record early: the inbound interest, the industry consolidation, the comparable transactions at strategic multiples. Make the buyer a fact the court finds, not an argument the court discounts.

Handled right, the strategic buyer does not just raise the number. It reframes the whole case — from a fight over a small company's earnings to a fight over what that company is worth to the market that wants it.
