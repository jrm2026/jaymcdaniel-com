Two engineers built a firm over eighteen years. The operating agreement they signed in 2008 splits the company evenly and binds each of them to a three-year restriction on competing in New Jersey. Neither has read that paragraph since.

One of them now wants out. The covenant is why the negotiation has a floor: the continuing owner is buying a business the departing owner cannot rebuild across town next quarter, and the price reflects it.

Senate Bill 1407 would void that paragraph on the day it is signed — not prospectively, but as to the covenant executed in 2008 — and require the firm to notify its departing owner within thirty business days that he is free to compete. New Jersey has been trying to pass some version of this for the better part of a decade, and the vehicles keep dying in committee. That history is the trap. The exposure is not that a ban is imminent; it is that when one arrives it reaches backward and takes effect immediately.

## What would the bill actually do?

S1407 supplements Title 34 and sorts every restricted person into one of two tiers.

For a worker who is not a senior executive, an employer may not seek, require, demand, or accept a covenant after the effective date, may not enforce one, and may not represent that the worker is subject to one. For a senior executive, an existing covenant is not grandfathered either: Section 4 makes it "void and not enforceable" unless conformed to twelve conditions — among them a twelve-month ceiling and, decisively, payment of one hundred percent of the worker's pay plus benefits for every month the restriction runs.

Two definitions decide the tier, and both cut against owners of closely held companies.

"Senior executive" requires *both* a policy-making position *and* total compensation of not less than **$151,164** in the year preceding the end of employment. Total compensation means "salary, commissions, nondiscretionary bonuses and other nondiscretionary compensation." A K-1 distribution is none of those. The owner who takes $130,000 in salary and $500,000 in distributions — an ordinary structure — appears to fall below the floor, landing in the fully retroactive tier alongside a first-year employee.

"Worker" is defined without regard to title and reaches anyone providing a service to the employer, paid or unpaid, expressly including supervisory and managerial personnel. A member who runs the business is a worker. The operating agreement's caption does not change that.

## What does the sale-of-business carve-out plainly cover?

Section 5 exempts a non-compete clause "entered into by an employer pursuant to a bona fide sale of a business entity, of the employer's ownership interest in a business entity, or of all or substantially all of a business entity's operating assets."

Two phrases do the work.

**"Pursuant to a bona fide sale."** The covenant must be given in connection with a sale. One signed at formation, on admission of a new member, or in an amended operating agreement adopted for governance reasons is not — and that is where most covenants binding owners of closely held New Jersey companies were signed.

**"By an employer... of the employer's ownership interest."** In the ordinary closely held transaction the seller is a member selling her own units; the entity sells nothing. Read literally, the most common business divorce in New Jersey does not fit the exception the bill wrote for business divorces. A court may supply the sensible construction. No one should build a buyout on the expectation that it will.

The common law drew this line by looking at substance. *Solari Industries, Inc. v. Malady*, 55 N.J. 571, 576 (1970), held that "a seller's incidental noncompetitive covenant, which is designed to protect the good will of the business for the buyer, is freely enforceable," while an employee's covenant is "not, because of the countervailing policy considerations, as freely enforceable." The bill converts that substantive inquiry into a textual one. It asks what the document is, not what the transaction did.

## Where does an owner-employee's covenant fall?

**The formation covenant.** Two equal members, both working full time, each drawing $140,000 in salary plus distributions, restricted by Article XI of a 2019 operating agreement. The clause bars a worker from operating a business after employment ends, and it arose out of an employment relationship that began the same day. No sale appears anywhere in the facts, so Section 5 is unavailable; salary sits below the floor, so the senior-executive tier is unavailable. Void, retroactively.

**The partial sale.** A member sells half her interest to her partner for $1.4 million, signs a five-year restriction as part of the deal, and stays on running the division she built. Both characterizations are available. There is a bona fide sale and real consideration — but the seller is a member rather than the employer, and because she remained employed the covenant also arises out of a continuing employment relationship. The buyer paid for goodwill assuming the restriction holds. Whether it holds turns on which characterization a court reaches first.

**The senior executive.** The managing member, $400,000 in salary, unambiguous policy-making authority. He is the one owner the bill appears to protect, and the protection is expensive: his covenant survives only if conformed to all twelve conditions within thirty business days, one of which requires the company to pay his full salary and benefits throughout the restricted period. A twelve-month restraint costs $400,000 to enforce. Most closely held companies will let it lapse, so the protective tier produces the same result as the tier that voids outright.

## What does retroactivity mean for a covenant already signed?

The prohibition reaches any attempt to "enforce a non-compete clause, **including a non-compete clause entered into before the effective date of this act**." Section 3 then requires clear and conspicuous notice, within thirty business days, that the covenant "will not be, and cannot legally be, enforced." The only temporal safe harbor is Section 5(b), preserving matters "[w]here a cause of action related to a non-compete clause accrued prior to the effective date." Accrual is the test, not filing — but a covenant no one has breached has produced no accrued cause of action, and the act takes effect immediately, so there is no window in which to generate one.

Then the exposure inverts. Attempting to enforce a void covenant becomes itself a violation, actionable for two years with liquidated damages, fees, and costs. The cease-and-desist letter that is standard practice today becomes the predicate for a claim against the company.

## What does a void covenant do to the number?

Consider a commercial brokerage with $6 million in revenue and $1.5 million of EBITDA, owned by three producers. Two of them personally hold the relationships behind roughly 55% of commission income. With enforceable covenants that book is transferable, and a buyer underwrites it at six times earnings — a $9 million enterprise.

Void the covenants and nothing about the operations changes. What changes is what the buyer is underwriting: the possibility that more than half of revenue can leave and immediately compete. He does not walk away. He reprices. At four and a half times, the same company is worth $6.75 million. The paragraph nobody had read since 2008 was carrying $2.25 million.

The same exposure surfaces where there is no buyer. Restrictive covenants are the mechanism that converts personal goodwill — value residing in an individual's relationships — into enterprise goodwill the company owns and can sell. Void them by statute and the conversion fails as a matter of law, which no drafting inside the operating agreement can cure. [Revenue Ruling 59-60](/authorities/revenue-ruling-59-60/), § 4.02(f), directs the appraiser to weigh the loss of a manager on whom the business depends and the absence of succession capacity. Whoever prepares the next buy-sell valuation or gift-tax filing has to ask whether the covenants are enforceable, and a negative answer feeds a key-person adjustment before any discount for control or marketability is reached. In a contested buyout that argument now arrives with a statute attached: what was an evidentiary fight about the strength of a covenant becomes a legal conclusion.

## Where should the covenant live instead?

Not in the operating agreement, and not alone.

Move the restraint into the transaction documents. When an owner exits, the covenant belongs in the purchase agreement, executed as part of the purchase of the interest, with consideration expressly allocated to it and recitals establishing that the buyer is acquiring goodwill the covenant protects. Section 5 is a transactional exception; give it a transaction to attach to.

Build the rest from restraints the bill treats as something other than a non-compete. Section 4(b) names three: agreements not to solicit or hire employees, agreements not to solicit or transact business with customers, clients, referral sources, or vendors, and nondisclosure agreements. That the bill offers them as alternatives is decent evidence the drafters did not intend to sweep them in, and they are the interests the common law has always protected. *Whitmyer Bros., Inc. v. Doyle*, 58 N.J. 25, 33 (1971), held that "the employer has no legitimate interest in preventing competition as such," but has "a patently legitimate interest in protecting his trade secrets as well as his confidential business information" and "an equally legitimate interest in protecting his customer relationships."

Draft them narrowly, because the bill's definition is functional: it reaches any agreement that "functions to prevent or hinder in any way" a worker from seeking work or operating a business. A non-solicitation clause covering every prospective customer in the state for five years is a non-compete wearing a different caption. Section 4(i) is the tell — it bars restricting a worker from serving a customer the worker did not solicit. The drafters drew their line at solicitation, not at service.

Delete the forfeiture provisions. Many operating agreements suspend deferred payments, redemption proceeds, or vested equity if a departing owner competes. The bill reaches any agreement that "penalizes a worker for" seeking work elsewhere, and a forfeiture is a penalty. That clause is as void as the covenant it enforces, and more dangerous, because it is buried in the payment waterfall where nobody looks for it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does the bill reach a covenant in an LLC operating agreement?**
Almost certainly, if the restricted member also works in the business. S1407 defines a non-compete clause as "any agreement arising out of an existing or anticipated employment relationship," including any "contractual term or workplace policy, whether written or oral," so the document holding it is irrelevant. Because "worker" covers anyone providing a service to the entity without regard to title, a member who manages or works in the business is covered. A genuinely passive member performing no services has an argument for falling outside the definition.

**Does it void a covenant I gave when I sold half my interest to my partner?**
Unresolved, and that is the exposure. Section 5(a) exempts a covenant "entered into by an employer pursuant to a bona fide sale of a business entity, of the employer's ownership interest in a business entity, or of all or substantially all of a business entity's operating assets." A member selling her own units is not the employer selling its ownership interest, and if she stayed on after closing the covenant also arises out of the continuing employment relationship. Document the sale and allocate consideration to the covenant — because if the exception fails, the buyer paid for goodwill the statute has just released.

**Do non-solicitation and confidentiality clauses survive?**
On the text, yes. Section 4(b) identifies customer non-solicitation, employee non-solicitation, and nondisclosure agreements as alternative arrangements capable of protecting an employer's legitimate business interests, which presupposes they are not themselves non-competes. That tracks *Whitmyer Bros., Inc. v. Doyle*, 58 N.J. 25, 33 (1971). The caveat is that the definition reaches anything that "functions to prevent or hinder in any way" a worker from working, so an overbroad non-solicit invites recharacterization.

**What happens to a case already pending?**
Section 5(b) provides that the act does not apply "[w]here a cause of action related to a non-compete clause accrued prior to the effective date." Accrual controls, not the filing date, so a breach occurring before enactment preserves the claim even if the complaint follows. A covenant not yet breached generates no accrued cause of action, and because the act takes effect immediately there is no period in which to create one.
