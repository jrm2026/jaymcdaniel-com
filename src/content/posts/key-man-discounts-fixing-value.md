A key-man discount reflects a simple risk: if the business would lose material value on the departure, disability, or death of one individual, a buyer pays less for it. The appraiser quantifies that risk and reduces value accordingly. The concept is sound. The fight is always about magnitude.

The discount sticks when the dependence is documented and structural. Revenue concentrated in relationships the key person owns personally. Technical or licensing capability that lives in one head. Customer loyalty that follows the individual, not the entity. Where those facts are on the record, a discount in the range appraisers actually apply is defensible.

It fails when it is asserted rather than shown, or when the dependence is curable. A management team in place, transferable customer contracts, documented processes, an earn-out or employment agreement that retains the key person through transition — each of these shrinks the discount, because each reduces the risk the buyer is being asked to bear.

For the owner arguing for the discount, the work is to prove the business cannot be easily separated from the person. For the owner arguing against it, the work is to prove it can. Either way, the number turns on evidence, not on the label.
