When Paul Mitchell died in 1989, he was the public face of the hair-care company that carried his name.  When the estate filed its return it reported the stock at $28.5 million. The IRS came back with $105 million. 

The gap between those two numbers was not a dispute about revenue or margins. It was a dispute about how much of the company walked out the door in the coffin.

The Tax Court took a $150 million enterprise value and cut it by ten percent — $15 million — for the loss of Mitchell's public presence and creativity, before it ever reached the discounts for lack of control and marketability. That ten percent is a key-man discount. The Ninth Circuit later vacated the valuation and sent it back, not because the discount was wrong, but because the court had not explained how it got there. *Estate of Mitchell v. Commissioner*, 250 F.3d 696 (9th Cir. 2001); *Estate of Mitchell v. Commissioner*, 74 T.C.M. (CCH) 872 (1997).

That is the whole subject in one case. The discount was real. Its size was the fight. And a number asserted without a record behind it does not survive review.

## What the discount is actually pricing

A key-man discount reflects a risk a buyer refuses to pay for: that the business loses material value if one person leaves, is disabled, or dies. The appraiser identifies the dependence, quantifies the earnings exposed to it, and reduces value to reflect the risk the buyer would otherwise bear alone. It is a going-concern adjustment, applied to the enterprise before the interest-level discounts, and it is grounded in the oldest valuation authority the IRS still uses.

[Revenue Ruling 59-60](/authorities/revenue-ruling-59-60/), § 4.02(f), says it plainly. The loss of the manager of a "one-man" business "may have a depressing effect upon the value of the stock of such business, particularly if there is a lack of trained personnel capable of succeeding to the management of the enterprise." The ruling directs the appraiser to weigh the effect of that loss on future earnings and the absence of management-succession potential. Sixty-five years later the analysis has not changed. The dependence and the succession gap are the two variables, and everything a good report does is put evidence under both.

The mechanics vary. Some appraisers take the discount as a percentage haircut to enterprise value, the way the Tax Court did in *Mitchell*. Others build it into the capitalization rate or the cash-flow projection, raising the company-specific risk premium or modeling the earnings decline a departure would cause. The method matters less than the discipline. A percentage pulled from the air is indefensible whether it lives in a discount line or a discount rate.

## Why it is never automatic

The discount sticks when the dependence is documented and structural. Revenue concentrated in relationships the key person owns personally, not the entity. Technical, creative, or licensing capability that lives in one head. Customer loyalty that follows the individual out the door. A rainmaker who originates the work everyone else executes. Where those facts are on the record — named accounts, personal guarantees, licenses held individually, a book of business built on one person's name — a discount in the range appraisers actually apply is defensible, and single-digit to low-double-digit percentages are the ordinary terrain.

It fails when it is asserted rather than shown. "The owner is essential" is a conclusion, not evidence, and it is the first thing to fall on cross-examination. The party seeking the discount has to prove the business cannot be separated from the person. Vague indispensability does not carry that burden; concentration data, org charts, and the customer list do.

*Mitchell* is the cautionary version even though the discount survived. The Tax Court believed the loss of Paul Mitchell mattered and priced it at ten percent. The Ninth Circuit still vacated, because the record did not explain why ten and not five or twenty. A discount you cannot trace to evidence is a discount an appellate court can erase.

## The cure defeats the discount

Every fact that makes the dependence curable shrinks the number, because each one reduces the risk the buyer is being asked to absorb.

A management team already running operations. Customer contracts that are assignable and not personal. Documented processes, a trained second tier, a real succession plan. An employment agreement or earn-out that retains the key person through transition, so the buyer gets the handoff he is paying to avoid losing. Key-person life insurance payable to the company, which converts the death risk into cash on the balance sheet. An enforceable non-compete agreement that stops the departing owner from taking the goodwill across the street.

Each of these is a partial answer to the question the discount asks. The owner arguing for the discount wants none of them in the record. The owner arguing against it builds the case out of exactly these facts: the person is important, but the business would survive his exit, and here is the infrastructure that proves it. The number turns on how much of the dependence money and planning have already cured — because whatever can be replaced at reasonable cost is not a discount, it is an expense, and it belongs in normalized earnings, not in a haircut to value.

## The doctrine that cuts the other way

Take the counterargument seriously, because in a buyout or a divorce it is often the winning one.

The key-man discount is a fair-market-value idea. It prices the risk a hypothetical buyer would bear, and it lives most comfortably in the world *Revenue Ruling 59-60* governs: gift tax, estate tax, anything the IRS reviews. Move the same company into a statutory fair-value proceeding and the ground shifts under it.

New Jersey values the enterprise as a going concern in an oppression or dissenters'-rights buyout, and it disfavors discounts that let a controlling owner buy the minority out for less than a proportionate share. [*Lawson Mardon Wheaton, Inc. v. Smith*](/authorities/lawson-mardon-wheaton-v-smith-160-nj-383/), 160 N.J. 383 (1999); [*Balsamides v. Protameen Chemicals, Inc.*](/authorities/balsamides-v-protameen-160-nj-352/), 160 N.J. 352 (1999). Delaware fixed the unit of valuation as the company itself, not the shares in a particular holder's hands, in [*Cavalier Oil Corp. v. Harnett*](/authorities/cavalier-oil-v-harnett-564-a2d-1137/), 564 A.2d 1137 (Del. 1989). A key-man discount asserted in that setting invites the objection that it is a control or marketability discount wearing a different label — a way to shave the minority's number for a risk the going-concern standard does not charge to him.

The sharper objection is double counting, and it is available in every forum. Competent valuation normalizes owner compensation: it replaces what the owner actually pays himself with what the market would pay a replacement to do the job. That normalization already accounts for the cost of the labor the owner supplies. Apply a key-man discount on top, and the report has charged for the same dependence twice — once by paying a market salary for a replaceable manager, and again by treating the manager as irreplaceable. Those two assumptions are in tension, and a good cross-examiner will make the expert choose one.

New Jersey's matrimonial goodwill cases run the same logic without ever using the phrase "key-man discount." [*Dugan v. Dugan*](/authorities/dugan-v-dugan-92-nj-423/), 92 N.J. 423 (1983), and [*Slutsky v. Slutsky*](/authorities/slutsky-v-slutsky-451-nj-super-332/), 451 N.J. Super. 332 (App. Div. 2017), hold that only the earnings a business produces beyond the market price of the owner's own labor are enterprise goodwill; the rest is personal, and it is the owner's earning capacity, not a distributable asset. Subtract a market salary and what survives is, by construction, value the enterprise generates independent of the individual. That subtraction is the same move as normalizing compensation, and it means the personal-versus-enterprise line and the key-man discount are two names for one question: how much of this company is the person, and how much is the thing he built?

## Same facts, three different answers

The standard of value decides whether the discount is even on the table, so the same dependence produces three different results depending on where the fight happens.

In estate and gift tax, fair market value governs and the key-man discount is squarely available. *Mitchell* is the model: the founder's death is priced directly into enterprise value, and the litigation is about magnitude and record, not availability.

In a fair-value buyout — oppression, dissent, a court-ordered purchase under [N.J.S.A. 14A:12-7(8)](/authorities/njsa-14a-12-7/) — the discount is disfavored and often collapses into the reasonable-compensation analysis. The dependence does not disappear, but it gets priced through normalized earnings and the going-concern premise rather than through a separate haircut, and an expert who bolts a key-man discount onto a fair-value number may see it stripped out.

In a matrimonial case, no interest is sold and discounts for illiquidity are generally inapplicable in the first place. [*Brown v. Brown*](/authorities/brown-v-brown-348-nj-super-466/), 348 N.J. Super. 466 (App. Div. 2002); [*Denike v. Cupo*](/authorities/denike-v-cupo-394-nj-super-357/), 394 N.J. Super. 357 (App. Div. 2007). What remains is the *Dugan* question, litigated as personal versus enterprise goodwill and decided on reasonable compensation. A performer whose value is entirely his own reputation, like the entertainer in [*Piscopo v. Piscopo*](/authorities/piscopo-v-piscopo-232-nj-super-559/), 232 N.J. Super. 559 (App. Div. 1989), is the extreme case: nearly all key person, almost no transferable enterprise.

The company is identical in all three. The standard of value is what moves the number, and picking the wrong forum's rule concedes value the standard does not require.

## What a defensible analysis contains

A key-man opinion that holds up does the same work whichever side prepares it, because both sides are arguing about evidence, not the label.

It measures the dependence instead of asserting it. Revenue by customer and by originator, showing concentration in the individual. Which relationships, licenses, and guarantees are held personally rather than by the entity. What the company has done to reduce the exposure — succession depth, insurance, contracts, covenants — and what it has not.

It quantifies the cost and time to replace, and it puts that cost where it belongs. The replaceable function is an expense captured in normalized compensation. Only the increment that cannot be cured at reasonable cost — the reputation, the personal book, the creative signature that no salary rehires — belongs in the discount. Reconciling the two is the analytical core, and the report that stacks them mechanically has not been reconciled at all.

It anchors the magnitude to something outside the appraiser's judgment. *Mitchell*'s ten percent is a data point; so are the company-specific risk premiums in the appraisal literature and the terms of comparable transactions that priced similar dependence. A number derived from evidence survives appeal. A number asserted does not, which is precisely why *Mitchell* came back.

And it reads the governing documents before it models anything. An employment agreement, a non-compete, a buy-sell that already addresses the departure, key-person insurance — each changes the risk, and a discount that ignores them is pricing a company that does not exist.

The question underneath is the one that recurs across every practice area where value is contested: what is this business worth when the person who built it walks out the door? In estate planning it sets the transfer-tax number. In a buyout it decides whether the minority is cashed out fairly or cheaply. In a divorce it separates the marital asset from the spouse's future paycheck. In succession planning it is the reason the deal is structured to keep the key person in the building long enough to make himself replaceable. Same question, four rooms, and the answer always turns on evidence rather than the strength of the adjective attached to the owner.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a key-man discount, and when does it apply?**
It is a reduction in the value of a closely held business to reflect the risk that the enterprise loses material value if one individual leaves, is disabled, or dies. It applies where the business's earnings genuinely depend on that person and the dependence cannot be cured at reasonable cost. [Revenue Ruling 59-60](/authorities/revenue-ruling-59-60/), § 4.02(f), recognizes that the loss of the manager of a "one-man" business can depress the value of its stock, especially where no trained successor exists.

**How large is a key-man discount?**
There is no fixed percentage. It turns on the degree of dependence and how much of it has been mitigated, and appraisers commonly land in the single digits to low double digits. In *Estate of Mitchell v. Commissioner*, 74 T.C.M. (CCH) 872 (1997), the Tax Court applied a ten percent discount for the loss of Paul Mitchell, but the Ninth Circuit vacated the valuation, 250 F.3d 696 (9th Cir. 2001), because the court had not explained how it reached the figure. Magnitude has to be tied to evidence, not a rule of thumb.

**Does a key-man discount apply in a shareholder or LLC buyout?**
The answer is very fact-specific, but usually the concept is different. New Jersey values the enterprise as a going concern in a fair-value buyout and disfavors discounts that benefit the controlling owner. [*Lawson Mardon Wheaton, Inc. v. Smith*](/authorities/lawson-mardon-wheaton-v-smith-160-nj-383/), 160 N.J. 383 (1999); [*Balsamides v. Protameen Chemicals, Inc.*](/authorities/balsamides-v-protameen-160-nj-352/), 160 N.J. 352 (1999). The dependence is typically priced through normalized compensation instead, and a separate key-man haircut layered on top invites a double-counting objection.

**Isn't a key-man discount just double counting if the appraiser already normalized compensation?**
It can be, and that may be the strongest attack on it. Normalizing compensation replaces the owner's pay with the market cost of a replacement, which already accounts for the labor he supplies. A key-man discount may only price the increment a replacement cannot restore — the personal relationships, reputation, or creative capacity no salary rehires. If the report both pays for a replaceable manager and discounts for an irreplaceable one, it has charged twice for the same person.

**How does this affect what my ownership interest is actually worth?**
It can move the number materially, in either direction, depending on the forum. In estate and gift tax the discount lowers the taxable value and can be worth pursuing aggressively with a contemporaneous appraisal. In a buyout you are more often defending against it, because it can shave a departing owner's fair-value price for a risk the going-concern standard does not charge to him. Either way the size is decided by evidence — concentration data, succession depth, insurance, and contracts — not by how indispensable the owner says he is.
