A pediatric practice in Bergen County had an operating agreement that told the members exactly what to do. Certify a value for the company. If more than two years passed without a new certificate, the buyout price for a retiring member would be built off the last agreed value, adjusted for changes in net worth.

The members signed a Certificate of Agreed Value on January 1, 2000, fixing the company at $2.4 million. They never signed another one.

Sixteen years later Dr. Namerow announced his retirement — the first retirement in the practice's history, and the first occasion anyone had to apply the clause. He argued that the periodic fair-market-value appraisals the members had obtained over the years had established a course of conduct modifying the agreement, and that enforcing a certificate from the millennium breached the fiduciary duties owed to him. Neither argument survived summary judgment. Where a contract is unambiguous, the court will not look outside its four corners, and a fiduciary duty claim cannot deliver a valuation methodology a member never bargained for. [*Namerow v. PediatriCare Associates, LLC*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/namerow-v-pediatricare-461-nj-super-133/), 461 N.J. Super. 133 (Ch. Div. 2018).

Nobody in that room in 2000 intended a payout frozen at the millennium. The clause did not fail because it was unfair. It failed because it attached no consequence to the thing the members were most likely to do, which was nothing.

That is the shape of every exit gap. Not a decision anyone made, but a decision nobody made, discovered on the one day it cannot be unmade.

## The multiple is a risk measure, not a constant

Owners generally believe the business is worth earnings times a multiple, and that the multiple belongs to the industry. It does not. It belongs to the company, and it is an inverse measure of two things: how likely the earnings are to continue, and how much of them a buyer can actually take delivery of.

That is not a private theory of finance. It is the framework the tax law has used for sixty-five years. [Revenue Ruling 59-60](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/revenue-ruling-59-60/), 1959-1 C.B. 237, directs the appraiser to a hypothetical willing buyer and a hypothetical willing seller, neither compelled and both reasonably informed, and to weigh the nature and history of the business, the economic and industry outlook, earning capacity, dividend capacity, goodwill and other intangibles, and comparable market data. It warns against mechanical formulas.

Read that list from a buyer's chair. Every item is a question about durability. Each of the three gaps below answers one of those questions badly, and each answer arrives as a lower multiple rather than as an objection anyone states out loud.

## Gap one: the value that leaves when the owner does

The first gap is the largest and the least visible, because from inside the company it looks like competence.

Arnold Strassberg spent decades building the wholesale distribution of super-premium ice cream to supermarkets, on the strength of his personal relationships with supermarket owners and a handshake understanding with the founder of Häagen-Dazs. He held 51 percent of Martin Ice Cream Company, a New Jersey corporation in Bloomfield, and the company enjoyed the benefit of all of it. When Häagen-Dazs bought the distribution rights for $1,430,340, the Commissioner argued the corporation was the seller and owed the tax.

The Tax Court said the corporation had never owned the assets. Strassberg "never entered into a covenant not to compete with petitioner or any other agreement — not even an employment agreement — by which any of [his] distribution agreements ..., relationships with the supermarkets, and ... distribution expertise became the property of petitioner." *Martin Ice Cream Co. v. Commissioner*, 110 T.C. 189, 207 (1998). Personal relationships of a shareholder-employee are not corporate assets when the employee has no contract with the corporation, and they are entirely distinct from corporate goodwill.

The corporation largely prevailed. Read the case as an exit-planning case and it is a warning. The company had operated for years on assets it did not own, and it had no way to sell them.

New Jersey draws the same line in matrimonial practice, which is where it is litigated most often. Goodwill is reputation likely to generate future business, and the portion that is distributable is the portion that exists as a going concern independent of the individual. [*Dugan v. Dugan*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/dugan-v-dugan-92-nj-423/), 92 N.J. 423 (1983). Absent an enforceable covenant, the practitioner takes the rest with him.

Where the person is staying but is irreplaceable, the same fact arrives as a discount instead. In [*Estate of Mitchell v. Commissioner*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/estate-of-mitchell-v-commissioner-250-f3d-696/), 250 F.3d 696 (9th Cir. 2001), the Tax Court had reduced a $150 million enterprise value by ten percent to reflect the loss of Paul Mitchell's public presence and creativity, taken against enterprise value before any shareholder-level discounts. The Ninth Circuit vacated on other grounds, including the Tax Court's failure to explain how it derived its figures, and left the concept intact.

Closing this gap is unglamorous and slow. A second layer of management with real decision authority. Client and referral relationships assigned to more than one person and documented as institutional. Covenants signed while everyone is still friendly. Compensation set at a defensible market rate, because the amount by which an owner overpays or underpays himself is the single largest input to what an appraiser will call enterprise value.

## Gap two: revenue that belongs to somebody else

Concentration is priced twice, and owners usually anticipate only the first.

The first pricing is the risk premium. A company whose top account is thirty or forty percent of revenue carries a loss the buyer cannot diversify away, so the buyer either raises the discount rate, lowers the multiple, or moves a large share of the price into contingent consideration. Concentration is a leading cause of earn-outs, and an earn-out trades a certain number today for an uncertain one tomorrow.

The second pricing is the harder one, and it is a question of ownership rather than risk. Whose customer is it?

[*Seiler v. Seiler*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/seiler-v-seiler-308-nj-super-474/), 308 N.J. Super. 474 (App. Div. 1998), is the cleanest illustration. The husband ran an exclusive Allstate agency. Allstate owned the equipment, assigned the telephone number, designed the advertising, and controlled hiring. The agency had goodwill and it was a real going concern. The goodwill belonged to Allstate. Nothing was distributable in the agent's hands.

The same structure recurs everywhere: franchisees on a tight leash, captive producers, subcontractors whose end customer contracts with the prime, distributors holding terminable-at-will territory rights. Substantial enterprise value exists, and none of it is the owner's to sell.

Between those poles sits the assignment clause, which is where most middle-market deals actually stall. A master services agreement that terminates or requires consent on a change of control gives the customer a veto over the transaction and a free option to renegotiate on the day the seller has the least leverage. Ten of those, in a business with concentrated revenue, is not a legal cleanup item. It is the price.

The work here is contractual. Written agreements with the anchor accounts, with terms long enough to survive a closing. Assignment provisions that permit transfer to an acquirer, or at minimum consent-not-unreasonably-withheld. Multiple relationship owners inside each account. And a deliberate program of broadening the base, which takes years and is the only one of these that cannot be lawyered.

## Gap three: the paper nobody maintained

The third gap is documentation, and it runs in two directions that owners tend to treat as unrelated.

The operational half is familiar from diligence. When how the business runs lives in habit rather than in systems — pricing decisions, vendor terms, quality control, the reason the second Tuesday matters — the buyer cannot test whether the earnings survive the owner's departure. Financial statements are the acute case. A company that has never been audited or reviewed, whose books mix personal and business spending, forces the buyer to fund a quality-of-earnings exercise and then to discount the result of it. Add-backs that the seller regards as obviously legitimate become negotiable, one line at a time, in the week before closing.

The governance half is *Namerow*, and it is worth more per hour of attention than anything else on this list.

An agreement that fixes the value of an interest is not merely evidence. In New Jersey it carries a presumption. Where a partnership or shareholder agreement fixes value, and it is established that the firm's books are well kept and the partners' interests are in fact periodically and carefully reviewed, that valuation may be attacked only on clear and convincing proof. [*Stern v. Stern*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/stern-v-stern-66-nj-340/), 66 N.J. 340 (1975).

Both halves of that sentence are doing work. The presumption protects the agreement that is maintained. It offers very little to the one signed years ago and never reopened — and, as *Namerow* shows, a stale certificate can still be enforced against the member it hurts, because unambiguous language does not become ambiguous by neglect.

So the annual valuation exercise the owners resent is the protection they believe they already have. Any clause keyed to a periodically certified value needs a stated fallback for the years the certificate does not get signed: an independent appraisal, an inflation adjustment, a formula, anything other than silence.

## The doctrine that cuts the other way

The honest counterargument is that readiness spending does not always pay back, and it deserves a straight answer.

Buyers price what they see, and a seller who spends three years and real money installing a management layer, papering customer contracts, and paying for audited statements may find the multiple moves less than the outlay. Concentration in a business with a genuinely sticky anchor customer may be worth less of a haircut than the textbook assumes. Some of what gets sold as exit planning is a fee-generating checklist.

Two responses. The first is that most of these gaps are not priced symmetrically. Buyers underwrite to downside, so an unquantifiable risk gets a larger deduction than the same risk quantified and bounded — which means the return on documentation is often in removing the uncertainty rather than in changing the underlying fact.

The second is *Namerow* again. The governance half of gap three costs a few hours a year and is the only one of the three where the loss is unbounded and entirely self-inflicted. A stale certificate does not reduce a multiple. It replaces the number outright.

And there is a real limit on the other side. Courts enforce agreements as written. A seller who neglects the paper for a decade and then argues that everyone understood something different is asking for the relief *Namerow* denied.

## What a readiness file actually contains

Start with the ownership documents, not the operations. Read the operating or shareholder agreement against the exit the owner actually intends, and identify which provision governs it. A functioning buy-sell displaces the statutory default for the transaction it covers, so the clause may be the whole answer.

Fix the compensation record before anything else in the financials. Normalized owner compensation is the hinge between what the company earns and what an appraiser will treat as enterprise earnings, and it needs to be defensible from payroll data rather than reconstructed later.

Convert relationships into contracts, then check the contracts for assignability. This is the difference between goodwill the buyer can own and goodwill the buyer is renting from whoever leaves.

Get covenants signed. Their absence is what *Martin Ice Cream* was about, and it is the cheapest value protection an owner will ever buy — cheapest precisely because it must be purchased before anyone is contemplating leaving.

Baseline the number now, with a real appraisal, and repeat it. The valuation is worth more as a diagnostic than as a figure. It tells the owner which gap is costing the most, and it builds the periodic-review record *Stern* rewards.

Then sequence it against the actual timeline. None of this closes in the ninety days before a letter of intent. Exit readiness is a planning discipline that happens to produce a transaction, not a transaction task that happens to require planning.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does owner dependence really reduce what a buyer will pay?** Yes, and it can do so in two different ways. Where the value is personal to the owner and unprotected by any agreement, it may not be the company's to sell at all — in *Martin Ice Cream Co. v. Commissioner*, 110 T.C. 189 (1998), the shareholder's customer relationships and distribution rights were never corporate assets because he had no employment agreement or covenant not to compete. Where the owner stays but is irreplaceable, the same fact arrives as a key-person discount against enterprise value. [*Estate of Mitchell v. Commissioner*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/estate-of-mitchell-v-commissioner-250-f3d-696/), 250 F.3d 696 (9th Cir. 2001).

**Our operating agreement sets a buyout price. Do I still need a valuation?** Yes, and probably annually. [*Stern v. Stern*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/stern-v-stern-66-nj-340/), 66 N.J. 340 (1975), gives an agreement's fixed valuation near-presumptive force only once it is established that the books are well kept and the interests are periodically and carefully reviewed. Without that record the presumption weakens, and [*Namerow v. PediatriCare Associates, LLC*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/namerow-v-pediatricare-461-nj-super-133/), 461 N.J. Super. 133 (Ch. Div. 2018), shows a stale certified value being enforced sixteen years after it was signed.

**How much does customer concentration cost me?** It costs twice — once in the discount rate or multiple the buyer applies to the risk that the account leaves, and again in deal structure, because concentration is a leading driver of earn-outs and escrows that shift the price into contingent consideration. Where the customer relationship legally belongs to someone else, the answer is worse than a discount: there may be no transferable value in the owner's hands at all. [*Seiler v. Seiler*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/seiler-v-seiler-308-nj-super-474/), 308 N.J. Super. 474 (App. Div. 1998).

**How far ahead of a sale should this start?** Two to three years for anything structural. Management depth, a diversified customer base, and audited or reviewed financial statements all require elapsed time to become credible — a buyer wants to see the second-tier managers running the business, not organized on a chart. The governance items are the exception and should be fixed immediately, because they cost almost nothing and are enforceable against the owner exactly as written.

**Is this different if I am selling to my partners rather than to the market?** The gaps are the same and the leverage is different. An internal buyer is typically constrained by financing rather than by opinion of value, and the price will usually be set by the agreement rather than by an appraisal — which returns the owner to *Namerow* and to whether anyone maintained the document. The valuation standard in the agreement also matters: fair market value with discounts, applied to a departing owner bought out by the only other owner, prices a control transfer as though it were the sale of a minority stub to a stranger.
