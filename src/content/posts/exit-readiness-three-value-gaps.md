Most owners assume the business is worth what it earns times a multiple, and the multiple is roughly fixed. It is not. The multiple is a measure of how risky and how transferable the earnings are, and three recurring gaps push it down — often without the owner noticing until a buyer's diligence team names them.

The first is owner dependence. When the relationships, decisions, and technical knowledge run through one person, the buyer is buying that person, not the company. A management layer, delegated authority, and documented decision rights convert owner value into enterprise value.

The second is customer concentration. A business where one or two accounts drive much of the revenue carries a risk the buyer prices heavily, because the loss of a single relationship can impair the whole investment. Diversifying the base, and locking in the anchor accounts with real contracts, narrows the gap.

The third is undocumented process. When how the business runs lives in habit rather than in systems, the buyer cannot be confident the earnings survive the transition. Written processes, clean financials, and transferable systems make the earnings look durable — which is what a higher multiple actually rewards.

None of the three closes in the ninety days before a sale. They close over years, which is why exit readiness is a planning discipline, not a transaction task.
