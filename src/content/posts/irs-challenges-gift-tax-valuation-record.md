Anne Petter inherited a large block of UPS stock, contributed roughly $22.6 million of it to a family LLC, and in March 2002 moved membership units to trusts for her two children — part gift, part sale on twenty-year notes — with simultaneous gifts of units to two community foundations. Her appraiser, a Seattle firm, valued the units at $536.20 each, applying a 46 percent non-marketability discount and a 13.3 percent net asset value adjustment. She reported the transfers on a Form 709 with a disclosure statement describing the structure and the appraisal.

The IRS audited and came back at $794.39 per unit — roughly forty-eight percent above the reported figure — and issued a notice of deficiency for $2,115,797. Two weeks before trial the parties stipulated a value of $744.74.

The appraisal, in other words, was substantially wrong.

The plan held anyway. The transfer documents assigned to each trust the number of units equal to a stated dollar amount "as finally determined for federal gift tax purposes," with the remainder to charity, and obligated the trusts to hand over additional units to the foundations if the value came in higher. The Service argued the additional transfer was contingent on the audit and so not deductible. The Ninth Circuit disagreed and allowed a charitable deduction for the additional units the foundations would receive: the number they were entitled to "was capable of mathematical determination from the outset, once the fair market value was known." *Estate of Petter v. Commissioner*, 653 F.3d 1012, 1019-20 (9th Cir. 2011).

Read that sequence carefully, because it inverts what most owners assume. The appraisal did not have to be right. It had to be honest about its method, disclosed on the return, and backstopped by a document that could absorb being wrong.

Most planning files are none of the three.

## What the examiner is actually testing

There is no mystery about the standard. [Revenue Ruling 59-60](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/revenue-ruling-59-60/), 1959-1 C.B. 237, has governed the valuation of closely held stock for sixty-five years. Fair market value is the price at which property would change hands between a hypothetical willing buyer and a hypothetical willing seller, neither under compulsion and both reasonably informed. The ruling directs attention to the nature and history of the business, the economic and industry outlook, book value, earning capacity, dividend capacity, goodwill and other intangibles, prior sales of stock, and comparable-company market data.

And it says something examiners quote and taxpayers forget: no single formula controls, and mechanical application of one is not valuation.

That is the vulnerability in the typical discount file. A report that reaches a 35 percent marketability discount by citing a shelf of restricted-stock studies and a benchmark range has not valued this interest in this entity. It has looked up a number. The studies establish that illiquidity is costly in general; they do not establish what it costs here, given these transfer restrictions, this distribution history, this buy-sell, and this holding period.

The discounts have to be traced back to the operating agreement that creates them. A minority discount rests on the specific voting, management, and removal provisions that deny this holder control. A marketability discount rests on the specific transfer restrictions, rights of first refusal, and absence of a redemption right. When the entity documents are generic and the discount is large, the mismatch is the examination.

## The record, not the number, is what survives review

[*Estate of Mitchell v. Commissioner*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/estate-of-mitchell-v-commissioner-250-f3d-696/), 250 F.3d 696 (9th Cir. 2001), is the cleanest statement of this, and it is instructive because the taxpayer's problem came from the court rather than from the Service.

Paul Mitchell, co-founder of John Paul Mitchell Systems, died in 1989. The estate valued its 49.04 percent block at roughly $28.5 million; the Commissioner asserted $105 million. The Tax Court started from a $150 million enterprise value, took a ten percent key-person discount to reflect the loss of Mitchell's public presence and creativity, then applied a combined 35 percent minority and marketability discount at the shareholder level and a further adjustment for pending litigation.

The Ninth Circuit vacated and remanded. Not because the key-person discount was improper — it was not — but because the Tax Court failed to shift the burden of proof to the Commissioner once the estate showed the deficiency notice rested on a mistaken controlling-interest premise, and because it failed to explain the methodology behind its figures, including how it arrived at the specific numbers it chose.

The lesson runs in both directions. A percentage asserted without support fails. So does a conclusion that reaches a result without showing its work. A number nobody can trace to a stated method is as vulnerable on review as one left out altogether, and that is as true of the appraisal in the taxpayer's file as of the findings in an opinion.

## Adequate disclosure buys a clock, and the clock is the asset

The most valuable thing a gift tax return does has nothing to do with the tax computed on it.

A gift adequately disclosed on a return starts the ordinary three-year period of limitations for gift tax purposes. A gift not adequately disclosed does not: the value remains open, and it can be revisited years later, including in the computation of the donor's estate. The disclosure requirements are set out in Treas. Reg. § 301.6501(c)-1(f), and they are specific — a description of the transferred property and the consideration received, the identity of and relationship between the parties, the trust terms or a description of the trust, and either a qualified appraisal or a detailed description of the method used to determine value, including the financial data and restrictions relied on.

That regulation is the reason a competent planner treats the Form 709 as a filing rather than as an afterthought. It also explains the disclosure statement Anne Petter attached to her return, describing the defined-value structure and stating that the appraisal was based on the underlying asset value with a 46 percent non-marketability discount and a 13.3 percent net asset value adjustment.

She disclosed the aggressive discount. She was still audited, and she still lost the valuation. She was not exposed to an indefinite revaluation.

Two practical points follow. Disclose the method even where the discount is uncomfortable, because a disclosure that conceals the aggressive part does not qualify and therefore does not run the clock. And file a return for a transaction the client believes is not a gift at all — a sale at appraised value to a family trust, a redemption, a recapitalization. The transfer with a reported value of zero is the one that stays open forever.

## The clause that makes being wrong survivable

An appraisal of a closely held interest is an opinion, and reasonable professionals will differ by wide margins. Planning that requires the opinion to be correct is planning that will fail some meaningful percentage of the time.

The structural answer is to define the transfer by value rather than by quantity. Assign the number of units equal to a stated dollar amount as finally determined for federal gift tax purposes, and direct the remainder somewhere that produces no additional gift tax — typically a public charity, a marital trust, or an incomplete-gift vehicle. If the Service revalues upward, the allocation shifts. The taxable gift does not.

*Petter* is the case that made this workable at the appellate level, though it is narrower than it is usually cited for. The Ninth Circuit held that the reallocation was not a transfer dependent on a precedent event under Treas. Reg. § 25.2522(c)-3(b)(1), and rejected the Service's "but for the audit" framing: the audit did not create the charities' entitlement, it revealed an entitlement that was fixed from the outset. Value "as finally determined for federal gift tax purposes" means fair market value under ordinary gift tax principles, not the figure the donor reported.

Note what the court did not decide. The Service had argued below, on the authority of *Commissioner v. Procter*, 142 F.2d 824 (4th Cir. 1944), that the formula and reallocation clauses were void as against public policy. It abandoned that argument on appeal, and the panel expressly declined to reach it — as the Fifth Circuit had in *Succession of McCord v. Commissioner*, 461 F.3d 614, 623 (5th Cir. 2006). The Tax Court's rejection of the objection stands below, but *Petter* is not appellate authority on it.

The underlying distinction is still the whole doctrine, and it is why the structure works. A clause that returns property to the donor when the Service prevails undoes the transfer, and *Procter* is the reason courts refuse to honor that. A clause that fixes the dollar value passing to each of several real transferees, one of which actually receives the property, is a description of what was given.

Three cautions. The mechanism only works if the receiving charity or trust is genuinely independent and actually receives the property; a foundation controlled by the family that never sees the extra units invites a different argument entirely. The Service has not acquiesced in the taxpayer-favorable line and continues to litigate variants. And this is federal circuit law, so where the donor resides matters to how much comfort the structure deserves.

## The doctrine that cuts the other way

Take the government's position at its strongest, because a great deal of family-entity planning is weaker than its proponents believe.

Discounts on an entity created weeks before a transfer, holding nothing but marketable securities, with no business operations and no purpose the family can articulate other than tax, are exactly what the anti-abuse provisions were enacted to address. Section 2703(a) directs that property be valued without regard to any option, agreement, or right to acquire it at less than fair market value, and without regard to any restriction on the right to sell or use it, unless the arrangement satisfies all three prongs of the § 2703(b) safe harbor: a bona fide business arrangement, not a device to transfer property to family members for less than full consideration, and terms comparable to those of an arm's-length arrangement. Section 2704(b) separately disregards certain applicable restrictions on liquidation in family-controlled entities.

Nothing in a well-prepared appraisal fixes a structure that fails those tests. The appraisal is downstream. If the restrictions generating the discount are disregarded by statute, the discount goes with them, and the quality of the report is beside the point.

The penalty regime sharpens this. Section 6662(g) imposes an accuracy-related penalty for a substantial estate or gift tax valuation understatement, and § 6662(h) doubles it for a gross misstatement, on thresholds keyed to how far the claimed value falls below the value finally determined. The relief valve is the reasonable cause and good faith exception in § 6664(c) — and the substance of a reasonable cause defense is a contemporaneous, independent, qualified appraisal that the taxpayer actually relied on. The same file that supports the number defends against the penalty for missing it, which is why an appraisal obtained after the notice of examination is worth so much less than one dated before the gift.

## What a defensible file contains

An appraisal dated before or contemporaneously with the transfer, by an independent credentialed appraiser with no role in the plan. Timing and independence are the first two things an examiner checks, and both are binary.

A stated standard of value and a stated method, with the steps visible. *Mitchell* is the authority for the proposition that unexplained figures do not survive review, and it applies to the report as much as to an opinion.

Discounts tied to the governing documents rather than to a range. Cite the section of the operating agreement that creates the restriction, quantify what that restriction does to a holder, and reconcile the empirical studies to the specific facts instead of averaging them.

Entity documents that predate the transfer and reflect a real arrangement. Capital accounts funded, formalities observed, distributions made or not made for stated reasons, and a business purpose the family can describe without using the word "estate."

A Form 709 that satisfies Treas. Reg. § 301.6501(c)-1(f), including the transactions the client does not think are gifts.

A transfer document that defines what passes by value rather than by count, with a real transferee taking the excess.

And an internal consistency check against everything else the family has said the company is worth. Buy-sell certificates, bank submissions, a matrimonial appraisal, an offer from a competitor, the valuation used for an ESOP or a partner admission. Examiners find those documents, and so does opposing counsel in the next dispute.

That last point is where this analysis leaves the tax file. The discount record built for the gift is the same record that will be read in a buyout or a divorce, where it will be used against the position the owner wants to take there. New Jersey does not permit minority or marketability discounts in an equitable distribution, and treats them as equitable and fact-specific in a statutory fair-value buyout. An aggressive discount file, once created, does not stay in the drawer it was built for.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long can the IRS challenge the value of a gift I already reported?** Indefinitely, unless the gift was adequately disclosed. Treas. Reg. § 301.6501(c)-1(f) sets out what a return must contain — a description of the property and the consideration, the parties and their relationship, trust terms where applicable, and either a qualified appraisal or a detailed description of the valuation method including the financial data and restrictions relied on. A gift that meets those requirements is subject to the ordinary limitations period; one that does not can be revalued years later, including at the donor's death.

**Are valuation discounts on a family entity still allowed?** Yes, when the restrictions producing them are real and respected. The constraint is statutory rather than evidentiary: § 2703(a) values property without regard to restrictions on sale or use unless the three-prong safe harbor in § 2703(b) is met, and § 2704(b) disregards certain applicable restrictions on liquidation in family-controlled entities. Where the entity holds only marketable securities and has no articulable purpose, the appraisal is not the problem and a better appraisal will not solve it.

**My appraiser and the IRS appraiser are millions apart. Does that mean somebody is wrong?** Not necessarily — it means the inputs differ, and the question on review is whether each side can trace its conclusion to a stated method. [*Estate of Mitchell v. Commissioner*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/estate-of-mitchell-v-commissioner-250-f3d-696/), 250 F.3d 696 (9th Cir. 2001), vacated a Tax Court valuation precisely because the court did not explain how it derived its figures. Under [Revenue Ruling 59-60](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/revenue-ruling-59-60/) there is no controlling formula, so the reasoning is the deliverable.

**Can a formula clause protect me if the value is adjusted upward?** It can, if it is drafted as a defined-value transfer rather than as a savings clause. In *Estate of Petter v. Commissioner*, 653 F.3d 1012 (9th Cir. 2011), documents assigning units worth a stated dollar amount "as finally determined for federal gift tax purposes" with the excess passing to charitable foundations produced a charitable deduction for the extra units after an upward revaluation, because the charities' entitlement was fixed from the outset rather than created by the audit. The Ninth Circuit did not reach whether such clauses are void as against public policy — the Service abandoned that argument on appeal. A clause that instead returns property to the donor if the Service prevails is the kind courts have refused to honor. *Commissioner v. Procter*, 142 F.2d 824 (4th Cir. 1944).

**What does a bad valuation actually cost me in dollars?** Additional gift tax on the revalued transfer, interest from the original due date, and potentially an accuracy-related penalty under § 6662(g), doubled under § 6662(h) for a gross valuation misstatement, computed on how far the reported value fell below the value finally determined. The defense is reasonable cause and good faith under § 6664(c), and in practice that means a contemporaneous, independent, qualified appraisal the taxpayer actually relied on — which is the same document that would have reduced the exposure in the first place.
