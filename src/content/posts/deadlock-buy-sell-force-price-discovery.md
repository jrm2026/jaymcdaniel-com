Deadlock is the failure of a two-owner company to make a decision it needs to make. Left alone, it destroys value — the business drifts while the owners fight, and a court-ordered dissolution sells the enterprise at the worst possible time. A properly drafted buy-sell is the private alternative, and its job is to force a price when the owners cannot agree on one.

The classic mechanism is the shotgun, or Texas, clause. One owner names a single price. The other must either buy at that price or sell at it. The discipline is elegant: the naming party cannot lowball, because it may be forced to sell at its own number, and cannot inflate, because it may be forced to buy. In theory, self-interest produces a fair price.

In practice, the clause rewards asymmetry. An owner with far greater liquidity can name a price the other cannot afford to match, forcing a sale below true value. The fix is structural — financing periods, installment terms, or an appraisal floor that keeps the mechanism from becoming a wealth transfer to the richer owner.

The lesson is that the buy-sell is not boilerplate. Drafted with attention to who holds the money and who holds the leverage, it turns deadlock into orderly price discovery. Drafted carelessly, it becomes the weapon the stronger owner uses on the weaker one.
