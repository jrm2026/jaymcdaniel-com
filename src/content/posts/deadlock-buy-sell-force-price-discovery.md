Fair value, a buy-sell price, and a liquidation price are three different numbers, and a deadlock can produce any of them. Which one governs is decided by the agreement and by who files first, not by what the business is worth. In New Jersey, a case pleaded as deadlock alone reaches the buyout statute stripped of the two provisions that move the number most.

Two men owned a restaurant on the Delaware shore and the land under it. They held the real estate through an LLC, fifty-fifty, and each had signed a personal guaranty for the full amount of the mortgage the company took out to buy the property. Then they stopped speaking. One voted to put the land on the market and to terminate the restaurant's lease. The other voted the other way. Nothing moved, which suited the man who preferred the status quo.

Their operating agreement had anticipated this. It contained an exit mechanism, and the party benefiting from the stalemate pointed to it: use the clause, take your money, go.

The Court of Chancery ordered the company dissolved instead. The exit mechanism was not an adequate remedy, Vice Chancellor Strine held, because nothing in it released the departing member from his guaranty. He would walk away still liable for the debt of an entity he no longer controlled. *Haley v. Talcott*, 864 A.2d 86 (Del. Ch. 2004).

Read that as a valuation case, which is what it is. The clause set a price. The price was wrong, not because the arithmetic was wrong, but because the mechanism moved only half of what an exit is supposed to move. A buy-sell that transfers equity while leaving liability behind has not priced the interest. It has priced part of it.

## Three machines, one of which is a valuation

A deadlocked owner sits inside one of three price-setting mechanisms, and they do not converge.

The first is the agreement. A shotgun clause, a formula, a stated appraisal process. What it produces is a bid, and a bid reflects what the bidder can pay and what he is willing to risk. It is not an opinion of value and was never built to be one.

The second is the court. A statutory buyout produces fair value, which measures the enterprise as a going concern and awards the owner a proportionate share of it. Delaware fixed that unit of valuation in [*Cavalier Oil Corp. v. Harnett*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/cavalier-oil-v-harnett-564-a2d-1137/), 564 A.2d 1137 (Del. 1989), and New Jersey and New York run the same way. [*Balsamides v. Protameen Chemicals, Inc.*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/balsamides-v-protameen-160-nj-352/), 160 N.J. 352 (1999); [*Friedman v. Beway Realty Corp.*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/friedman-v-beway-realty-87-ny2d-161/), 87 N.Y.2d 161 (1995).

The third is the market. Dissolution, a court-supervised sale, an auction the parties do not control.

The gap between the first two is the whole game. A shotgun price is constrained by the offeror's checkbook. Fair value is constrained by no one's. On the same company, on the same day, those numbers routinely differ by more than everything else in dispute.

## What a shotgun clause actually prices

The mechanism is elegant on paper. One owner names a single price. The other elects to buy at that price or sell at it. The offeror cannot lowball, because he may be forced to sell at his own number, and cannot inflate, because he may be forced to buy. Self-interest is supposed to produce something close to value.

It does, under one assumption that is almost never true in a closely held company: that both owners can perform.

Strip that assumption and the clause stops discovering price and starts discovering liquidity. The owner with capital names a number below value, knowing the other cannot fund a purchase at any price. The response is forced. Two identical fifty percent interests are economically different instruments when one holder can write the check and the other cannot, and the clause converts that difference into money.

Information runs the same direction. The owner who runs the business knows the receivables, the pipeline, the customer about to leave. The passive owner names a price into the dark. Where one side controls the books, a mechanism requiring the other side to price the company first is not a coin flip. It is a sale to the informed party at a number the uninformed party chose.

Then there is what each owner stands to lose. The owner whose livelihood, salary, and identity are the business will overpay to keep it. The owner who can walk will not. That asymmetry is invisible in the clause and decisive in its operation.

*Haley* is the fourth version of the same problem. The mechanism was symmetric in form and asymmetric in consequence, because it left the exiting member carrying the entity's debt. A court asked whether that constituted an adequate remedy said no, and dissolved the company rather than enforce the exit.

## New Jersey treats a pure deadlock claim as the weaker one

This is the part practitioners miss, and it is worth real money.

New Jersey's involuntary dissolution statute lists three separate grounds. Shareholders so divided in voting power that they have failed to elect successor directors across two consecutive annual meetings. Directors unable to effect action on one or more substantial matters respecting management of the corporation's affairs. And, in a corporation with twenty-five or fewer shareholders, conduct by those in control that is fraudulent, illegal, mismanaging, an abuse of authority, or oppressive or unfair toward a minority shareholder. [N.J.S.A. 14A:12-7(1)](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/njsa-14a-12-7/)(a), (b), (c).

All three open the same door. On any of them the court may appoint a custodian, appoint a provisional director, order a sale of stock, or dissolve.

The buyout provision is where they stop being equivalent. Under § 14A:12-7(8), on motion of the corporation or any shareholder party, the court may order a sale of another party's shares if it determines in its discretion that the order would be fair and equitable to all parties under all the circumstances. Purchase price is fair value as of the commencement of the action or such earlier or later date deemed equitable by the court, "plus or minus any adjustments deemed equitable by the court if the action was brought in whole or in part under paragraph 14A:12-7(1)(c)."

Read the condition. The equitable adjustment travels with the oppression ground. By its terms it is unavailable in a case pleaded only under (1)(a) or (1)(b).

Subsection (8)(d) does the same thing with fees. The court may award the selling shareholder reasonable fees and expenses of counsel and of any experts, including accountants, only "if the court finds that the action was maintainable under paragraph 14A:12-7(1)(c)." A pure deadlock case carries its own expert costs, and in a contested valuation those run into six figures.

Two claims that open the same door produce materially different economics once through it. The deadlock plaintiff gets fair value. The oppression plaintiff gets fair value, plus whatever the court finds equitable to add, plus a path to shifting the cost of proving it.

Deadlock is also the easier case to prove, which is exactly why it gets pleaded alone. Directors unable to act on substantial matters is close to self-proving in a fifty-fifty stalemate. Oppression requires evidence of conduct and frustrated expectations, and it is slower and more expensive to establish. The temptation is to plead the clean claim and skip the messy one.

That is a valuation decision wearing the costume of a pleading decision. Where the facts support both, plead both, and understand that the oppression count is not there for its own sake. It is there because it carries the adjustment and the fee shift.

## The LLC has no buyout election at all

New York's [Business Corporation Law § 1118](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/ny-bcl-1118/) lets the corporation or the non-petitioning shareholders elect to purchase at fair value, cutting off dissolution and ordinarily fixing value as of the day before the petition. That is an entitlement.

New Jersey's § 14A:12-7(8) is not. The statute says the court "may" order the sale, and only if it determines in its discretion that the order is fair and equitable to all parties. A New Jersey majority moving to buy out a deadlocked co-owner is asking, not electing.

For limited liability companies there is no buyout provision in the dissolution statute at all. [N.J.S.A. 42:2C-48](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/njsa-42-2c-48/)(a)(4)(b) supplies the deadlock ground, framed as whether it is reasonably practicable to carry on the company's activities in conformity with the certificate of formation and the operating agreement. Subsection (a)(5)(b) supplies the oppression ground. Subsection (b) authorizes a remedy other than dissolution, including a custodian or provisional managers, on a list that is expressly not exhaustive. Broad discretion, and no right to buy anyone out. The dissolution standard itself, and how narrowly courts read it, is treated at length in [the reasonably practicable standard](https://www.thebusinessdivorcelawyer.com/dissolution-and-the-reasonably-practicable-standard/) and in the [multistate remedy map](https://www.thebusinessdivorcelawyer.com/llc-minority-oppression-remedies-by-state/).

That absence is leverage, and it runs to the party willing to lose the company. The alternative to a negotiated price in a deadlocked New Jersey LLC is a court-supervised disposition of the business. If a credible acquirer exists, the disposition is not a fire sale. It is an auction the controlling member may lose, or may win only by outbidding the party who values the company most. That changes what the controlling member is actually deciding. Reservation prices move a long way when the question shifts from what will the judge award to what does control cost. The same mechanism drives [how a strategic buyer sets fair value in a buyout](https://jaymcdaniel.com/how-the-strategic-buyer-or-hypothetical-buyer-sets-fair-value-in-a-buyout/).

## The doctrine that cuts the other way

Take the counterargument seriously, because in most deadlocks it wins.

Courts enforce buy-sell agreements. In [*Namerow v. PediatriCare Associates, LLC*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/namerow-v-pediatricare-461-nj-super-133/), 461 N.J. Super. 133 (Ch. Div. 2018), a retiring physician sought a fair market valuation of the practice. The operating agreement specified a net worth methodology keyed to the last value the members had agreed on, and the members had not updated it in years. The court enforced the provision as written. Sixteen years of contrary course of conduct did not modify it, and a fiduciary duty claim could not be used to reach a methodology the plaintiff had not bargained for.

New Jersey has taken that position for half a century. In [*Stern v. Stern*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/stern-v-stern-66-nj-340/), 66 N.J. 340 (1975), the Supreme Court held that where a partnership or shareholder agreement fixes the value of an interest, and the firm's books are well kept and the interests periodically and carefully reviewed, that valuation may be displaced only by clear and convincing proof. The corollary matters more than the rule: an agreement whose values are stale and never reviewed is far more vulnerable than one maintained on a schedule. Owners who resent the annual valuation exercise are declining to buy the protection they think they already have.

*Haley* is the exception, and it is narrower than practitioners want it to be. Strine did not hold that an exit clause producing a low price is inadequate. He held that a clause leaving the exiting member exposed on a guaranty for an entity he no longer controlled does not equitably effect the separation of the parties. Price alone will not get there. Structural failure to separate might.

Deadlock is also not oppression, and pleading it as though it were invites dismissal of the count that carries the money. New Jersey remains committed to majority rule in corporate governance, and disagreement among owners is not itself a statutory violation.

Then the sharpest one. In a New Jersey deadlock, conduct prices the outcome. [*Wisniewski v. Walsh*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/wisniewski-v-walsh-2015-nj-super-unpub-lexis-3001/), 2015 N.J. Super. Unpub. LEXIS 3001 (App. Div. Dec. 24, 2015), sustained a marketability discount against a selling shareholder whose own conduct had driven the litigation. Nothing about the marketability of a trucking company changes based on how a shareholder behaved. The discount was doing equitable work under an economic label, which is a fair criticism and beside the point for planning purposes. The point is that the record on who caused the impasse is a valuation record, and the owner who behaves badly during a deadlock may pay for it in the discount rather than in a finding.

## Time is a term of the deal

Every month a deadlocked company spends in litigation is a month of deferred capital expenditure, delayed hiring, customers who notice, and employees who update their resumes. That is not atmosphere. It is an input to the number, and the valuation date determines who absorbs it.

Section 14A:12-7(8)(a) makes the date a variable rather than a constant: the commencement of the action, or such earlier or later date deemed equitable. Whoever files sets the default. Whoever can outlast the other captures the difference between the company as it was and the company as it will be when the machinery finishes.

The statute also gives the court control of how the money moves. Under (8)(e), payment may be cash, notes, other property, or a combination, in the court's discretion, with cash preferred whenever practicable, and the court fixes whether a note is secured and at what rate. Under (8)(f), once the purchasing party posts adequate security, the selling shareholder loses all rights and status except the right to receive the price. A seller who wins on value and loses on terms has won an unsecured receivable from the person he just finished litigating against.

## Drafting so the clause discovers price instead of liquidity

The fixes are unglamorous, and they are the difference between a mechanism and a weapon.

Give the responding party real financing. A clause requiring cash at closing in thirty days belongs to whoever has cash. Installment terms, a defined note with security, and a financing contingency of meaningful length restore the responding party's ability to perform, which is the assumption the entire mechanism rests on.

Put a floor under the number. An independent appraisal establishing a minimum, or a collar around a range, prevents the naming party from exploiting an information advantage without eliminating the clause's discipline.

Equalize information before anyone names a price. Require delivery of current financials, a customer schedule, and pending contracts as a condition precedent to triggering. An owner who must price the company should be permitted to see it.

Move the liabilities with the equity. Release or indemnification of personal guarantees, and a covenant to refinance secured debt within a fixed period, are what *Haley* was actually about. Without them the mechanism does not separate the parties, and a court may say so.

Decide who may pull the trigger and when. A clause available only after a defined deadlock event, certified in writing, with a cooling-off period, is a price discovery device. A clause available at any time to either owner is an option one of them holds against the other.

Name the standard of value if the mechanism falls back to appraisal. Most agreements say fair market value because it sounds neutral. In a two-owner company where the departing owner's interest is bought by the only other owner, fair market value with discounts prices a control transfer as though it were the sale of a minority stub to a stranger. The buyer captures the discount. Nobody intended that at signing, and by the time it matters nobody can renegotiate it. The difference between the two standards, and what it is worth, is the subject of [fair value versus fair market value](https://jaymcdaniel.com/fair-value-v-fair-market-value-understand-the-differences/).

And review the values on a schedule. *Stern* protects the agreement that is maintained. It offers considerably less to the one signed in 2009 and never opened again.

## Sequencing, and where this breaks

Read the agreement before theorizing about the statute. If a functioning buy-sell covers the triggering event, *Namerow* is the likely outcome and the litigation is about the mechanism, not the company's worth.

Plead both grounds where the facts allow. The equitable adjustment and the expert fee shift under § 14A:12-7(8) are conditioned on the oppression ground, and pleading deadlock alone concedes both.

Build the conduct record from the first month. *Wisniewski* means the story of who broke the company is admissible against the number, and the party who documents the other side's obstruction is building a valuation exhibit.

Do not trigger a shotgun clause you cannot fund. The mechanism is binary and indifferent to your reasons. Model both outcomes at the price you intend to name, and if you cannot live with one of them, do not name it.

Move on the valuation date deliberately. Filing sets it, and in a deteriorating business the party who files early is choosing to be valued as of a better day.

Expect the counter-move. A sophisticated adversary responds to a deadlock petition by moving under § 14A:12-7(8) to convert the case into an appraisal, where the pressure of an uncertain outcome disappears and the fight narrows to two experts and a discount rate. Whoever controls the timing controls a meaningful share of the value.

A deadlock is not a stalemate. It is an auction with an uncertain number of bidders, conducted under rules the parties mostly wrote themselves and then forgot. The owner who understands which rules apply, and when they can still be changed, is the one who sets the price.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a shotgun or Texas clause enforceable?** Generally yes, as a contract, and courts do not rescue a party from a mechanism he agreed to. The exception is structural rather than economic. In *Haley v. Talcott*, 864 A.2d 86 (Del. Ch. 2004), the Court of Chancery held an exit mechanism inadequate and dissolved a fifty-fifty LLC because the clause did not release the departing member from his personal guaranty of company debt. A low price is not a defense. A mechanism that fails to separate the parties may be.

**Does the price produced by our buy-sell equal fair value?** No, and it was not designed to. A buy-sell price is a bid, constrained by the bidder's liquidity and risk tolerance. Fair value under [N.J.S.A. 14A:12-7(8)](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/njsa-14a-12-7/) is a proportionate share of the enterprise as a going concern. Where the agreement covers the triggering event, the agreement's number usually governs anyway. [*Namerow v. PediatriCare Associates, LLC*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/namerow-v-pediatricare-461-nj-super-133/), 461 N.J. Super. 133 (Ch. Div. 2018).

**Can a New Jersey court order a buyout in a deadlock case?** Yes, but no one is entitled to one. Section 14A:12-7(8) says the court may order a sale if it determines in its discretion that the order is fair and equitable to all parties. That differs from New York's [Business Corporation Law § 1118](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/ny-bcl-1118/), which gives the corporation and non-petitioning shareholders an election as of right. For limited liability companies, [N.J.S.A. 42:2C-48](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/njsa-42-2c-48/) contains no buyout provision at all, though subsection (b) authorizes remedies other than dissolution on a non-exhaustive list.

**Why plead oppression if deadlock is easier to prove?** Because the money is attached to the oppression ground. Under § 14A:12-7(8)(a), the court may adjust fair value up or down as equity requires only if the action was brought in whole or in part under § 14A:12-7(1)(c), and under § 14A:12-7(8)(d), counsel and expert fees may be shifted only if the action was maintainable under that same paragraph. A case pleaded as pure deadlock reaches the buyout without either.

**What does a deadlock do to the value of my interest?** It reduces it while it lasts, and the valuation date decides who bears the loss. Deferred investment, customer attrition, and employee departures all reach the earnings a court or appraiser will capitalize, and § 14A:12-7(8)(a) fixes value as of the commencement of the action or such other date the court finds equitable. Conduct matters as well: in [*Wisniewski v. Walsh*](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/wisniewski-v-walsh-2015-nj-super-unpub-lexis-3001/), 2015 N.J. Super. Unpub. LEXIS 3001 (App. Div. Dec. 24, 2015), a marketability discount was sustained against the shareholder whose behavior drove the litigation.
