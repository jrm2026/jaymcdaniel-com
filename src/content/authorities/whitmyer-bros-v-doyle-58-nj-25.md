If *Solari Industries, Inc. v. Malady* established that a New Jersey court will enforce a restrictive covenant to the extent reasonable, *Whitmyer* decided what "reasonable" is allowed to protect — and the answer is narrower than most agreements assume.

The Court began by subtraction. "The employer has no legitimate interest in preventing competition as such," a proposition it tied to the policy behind New Jersey's then-new Antitrust Act. What an employer may protect is a short list: its trade secrets, its confidential business information, and its customer relationships. The Court then trimmed even that. Matters of general knowledge within the industry "may not be classified as trade secrets or confidential information entitled to protection," and "routine or trivial differences in practices and methods" will not support restraining a former employee's competition. 58 N.J. at 33-34.

The second half of the opinion matters as much for planning. Trade secrets and confidential information are protected without any covenant at all — the Court cited *Sun Dial Corp. v. Rideout*, 16 N.J. 252 (1954), for the point — and observed that a reasonably confined noncompetitive agreement serves mainly to avoid difficulties of definition and proof. A departing owner is therefore never as free as a buyer's expert tends to assume, and the covenant's real function is evidentiary economy rather than the creation of a right from nothing.

For a closely held owner the practical reading is that scope decides enforceability. A broad restraint on a departing minority owner with no trade secrets and no customer contact is unlikely to survive *Whitmyer*, and valuing a company as though such a covenant were fully effective overstates the number in the same way the absence of any covenant understates it. The durable restraint is the narrow one *Solari* itself sketched: a limited restriction on dealing with the actual and prospective customers the departing owner substantially served, for a defined period.
