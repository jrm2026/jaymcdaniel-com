*Stern* is where the New Jersey goodwill line begins, and it begins by subtraction. A partner in a well-known firm challenged the treatment of his earning capacity as a distinct item of marital property, and the Court agreed with him: earning capacity, "even where its development has been aided and enhanced by the other spouse," is not property under N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23. It informs what distribution is equitable and bears more heavily on alimony, but it is not an asset. In a footnote the Court added that the goodwill of a law firm, though it could not ethically be sold, might still be proved to exist as "a real element of economic worth." That footnote is the opening *Dugan* walked through eight years later.

The second contribution is the one matrimonial counsel most often miss. Where a partnership or shareholder agreement fixes the value of an interest, *Stern* attached a presumption: once it is established that the firm's books are well kept and that partners' interests are in fact periodically and carefully reviewed, the agreement's valuation "should be subject to effective attack only upon the submission of clear and convincing proofs." A well-run firm's formula is therefore not merely evidence of value — it is close to a presumption of value.

That cuts both ways, and it is leverage either way. Counsel for the titled spouse should build the record on bookkeeping quality and periodic review before the appraisers ever testify. Counsel attacking the formula has to meet a clear-and-convincing standard, not a preponderance, and needs a concrete theory of why the formula is not objective — usually that it was designed to suppress payouts to departing partners rather than to measure value.
