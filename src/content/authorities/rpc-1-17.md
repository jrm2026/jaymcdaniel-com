New Jersey adopted RPC 1.17 in 1992, and it displaced a premise the professional-goodwill cases were built on. A lawyer or law firm may now sell a law practice, or an area of practice, good will included, subject to conditions that protect clients — written notice, the client's right to retain other counsel or take the file, and limits on how fees are handled. Admission to or withdrawal from a partnership is expressly *not* a "sale" for purposes of the rule.

The doctrinal effect on *Dugan* is probably modest. *Dugan* held that the unsalability of a law practice's good will did not defeat its existence as distributable property; if unsalability did not defeat goodwill, salability does not disturb the holding. The evidentiary effect is larger. *Stern*'s footnote and *Dugan* rested on advisory ethics opinions from the 1960s holding that a law practice's good will could not be sold; RPC 1.17 supersedes that premise. Transactions in professional practices now happen, and an appraiser who can anchor a capitalization factor to observed sales stands on firmer ground than one deriving it from the *Dugan* factors alone. Expect the argument before the case law catches up to it.
