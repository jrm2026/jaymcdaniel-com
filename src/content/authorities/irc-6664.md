Section 6664(c) is the relief valve on the accuracy-related penalty, and it is the provision that converts a well-built valuation file from good practice into a defense.

The rule is short: no penalty is imposed under section 6662 or 6663 with respect to any portion of an underpayment if it is shown that there was reasonable cause for that portion and that the taxpayer acted in good faith with respect to it. The burden sits with the taxpayer, and the determination is made on all the facts and circumstances rather than by any checklist.

Treas. Reg. § 1.6664-4 supplies the standard, and two points in it are worth an owner's attention. The most important single factor is the extent of the taxpayer's effort to assess the proper tax liability — which is a question about process and contemporaneous record, not about whether the number turned out to be right. And reliance on the advice of a professional adviser or on an appraisal does not by itself establish reasonable cause and good faith. It is evidence, weighed with everything else, and it is stronger where the appraiser was independent, credentialed, engaged before the transfer, and given complete information.

The practical consequence is that the defense is assembled at the same moment as the transfer or not at all. A qualified appraisal dated before the gift, from someone with no role in the plan, working from the entity's real documents and financial data, is what a reasonable cause showing looks like in a valuation case. An appraisal commissioned after the notice of examination reads as reconstruction, and it is being offered to excuse a penalty measured by exactly the gap it was hired to close. Read this section together with [I.R.C. § 6662](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/irc-6662/), which sets the penalty it excuses.
