The most valuable thing a gift tax return does has nothing to do with the tax computed on it. It starts a clock, and this regulation is what decides whether the clock runs.

A gift adequately disclosed on a return is subject to the ordinary period of assessment under section 6501. One that is not adequately disclosed is not: the value stays open, and it can be revisited years later, including in the computation of the donor's estate, when the appraiser has retired and the contemporaneous evidence has thinned. Paragraph (f)(2) sets out what adequate disclosure requires — a description of the transferred property and any consideration received by the transferor; the identity of, and relationship between, transferor and transferee; a description of the terms of any trust involved; either a detailed description of the method used to determine fair market value, including the financial data and the restrictions on the transferred interest that were taken into account, or an appraisal satisfying paragraph (f)(3); and a statement describing any position taken that is contrary to any proposed, temporary, or final regulation or revenue ruling published at the time of the transfer. The regulations were promulgated by T.D. 8845.

The standard the disclosure is measured against is functional: it must be sufficiently detailed to alert the Commissioner to the nature of the transaction, so that a decision whether to select the return for examination can be a reasonably informed one. The Tax Court has held that substantial compliance with paragraph (f)(2) suffices, which is a meaningful protection — but it is not a reason to draft toward the minimum.

Two practical points follow for an owner making a discounted transfer. Disclose the aggressive part. A disclosure that describes the transfer while obscuring the discount does not qualify, and therefore does not run the clock, which is the precise opposite of what the planning was for — *Estate of Petter v. Commissioner*, 653 F.3d 1012 (9th Cir. 2011), is the model here, where the return carried a statement describing the defined-value structure and stated the 46 percent marketability discount and 13.3 percent net asset value adjustment on its face. And file a return for the transaction the client believes is not a gift at all: a sale at appraised value to a family trust, a redemption, a recapitalization. The transfer nobody reported is the one that stays open forever.
