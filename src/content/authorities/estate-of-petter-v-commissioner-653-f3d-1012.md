*Petter* is the case to read before assuming a gift-tax appraisal has to be right. Anne Petter inherited a large block of UPS stock, contributed roughly $22.6 million of it to a family LLC, and in March 2002 moved membership units to trusts for two of her children — part gift, part sale on twenty-year notes — coupled with simultaneous gifts of units to two community foundations. Her appraiser valued the units at $536.20 each, applying a 46 percent non-marketability discount and a 13.3 percent net asset value adjustment. The IRS audited and came back at $794.39 per unit, issuing a notice of deficiency for $2,115,797. Two weeks before trial the parties stipulated $744.74.

The appraisal, in other words, was substantially wrong, and the plan survived anyway. The transfer documents assigned to each trust the number of units equal to a stated dollar amount — in the sale documents, "as finally determined for federal gift tax purposes" — with the remainder to the foundations, and obliged the trusts to hand over additional units to the foundations if the value came in higher. Her planner called the structure a "charitable freeze": if the Service successfully challenges the valuation, the amount of the undervaluation goes to charity rather than to the government as gift tax.

The Ninth Circuit's actual holding is narrower than the case is often cited for, and the distinction matters. The Service argued that because the foundations would not have received the extra units but for the audit, the additional transfer was dependent on a condition precedent and therefore not deductible under Treas. Reg. § 25.2522(c)-3(b)(1). The court rejected the "but for" test: the number of units the foundations were entitled to "was capable of mathematical determination from the outset, once the fair market value was known," so the audit did not create the entitlement, it revealed it. Value "as finally determined for federal gift tax purposes" means fair market value under ordinary gift tax principles, not the number the donor happened to report. The charitable deduction was allowed.

What the court did **not** decide is the part practitioners most often attribute to it. The Service had argued before the Tax Court, relying on *Commissioner v. Procter*, 142 F.2d 824 (4th Cir. 1944), that the formula and reallocation clauses were void as against public policy. It abandoned that argument on appeal, and the Ninth Circuit expressly declined to address it — the same course the Fifth Circuit took in *Succession of McCord v. Commissioner*, 461 F.3d 614, 623 (5th Cir. 2006). The Tax Court's rejection of the public-policy objection stands below, but *Petter* is not appellate authority on it.

For a closely held owner the planning lesson survives the narrowing. Define the transfer by value rather than by quantity, direct the excess to a transferee that generates no additional tax, and make sure that transferee is genuinely independent and actually receives the property. Then treat the appraisal as what it is — an opinion that may be revised — and build the structure so that a revision reallocates rather than taxes. The discipline that supports the number is separate, and it runs through *Revenue Ruling 59-60* and *Estate of Mitchell v. Commissioner*.
