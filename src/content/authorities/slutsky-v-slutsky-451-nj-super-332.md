*Slutsky* is the modern professional-goodwill case, and it is the one matrimonial counsel most often cite for the wrong proposition. The husband's expert had never said the firm lacked goodwill; he said this partner had none beyond his compensation, because a non-originating partner paid the market rate for his work has no excess earnings to capitalize. A finding that the enterprise enjoys reputation establishes nothing about that. Firm goodwill is not partner goodwill — they are different questions, and the trial court had answered only the easy one.

On remand the panel required findings on the threshold excess-earnings question, an evaluation of the shareholder agreement as a measure of firm value including goodwill, and a realistic projection of the partner's remaining term rather than an assumption of equity participation to the maximum age. Then the instruction that is easy to miss: invoking the catch-all equitable-distribution factor, N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23.1(p), the court directed that "a measure of consideration must be given to the lack of intrinsic value associated with any amount determined as individual goodwill." That is statutory authority to award the non-titled spouse less than half of an asset the titled spouse can never sell and must fund with cash — an answer to the equity problem *Dugan* named in 1983 and left open for thirty-four years.

*Slutsky* also reaffirmed *Stern*'s clear-and-convincing presumption for a firm's valuation formula and directed the trial court to test the objectiveness and accuracy of that formula. The practical consequence is blunt: after *Slutsky*, imputing firm-level goodwill to an individual partner is reversible error, not a weak inference.
