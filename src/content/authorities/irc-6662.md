Section 6662 is what turns a valuation disagreement into a number with a multiplier on it, and it is the reason the quality of the appraisal file matters beyond the tax on the transfer itself.

The structure is straightforward. Subsection (a) imposes a penalty of 20 percent of the portion of an underpayment attributable to one of the causes enumerated in subsection (b), and subsection (b) includes any substantial estate or gift tax valuation understatement. Subsection (g) defines that understatement by reference to how far the reported figure falls below the figure ultimately determined: the value claimed on the return must be 65 percent or less of the amount determined to be correct, and a de minimis threshold keeps small discrepancies out of the provision. Subsection (h) raises the stakes, substituting 40 percent for 20 percent where the portion of the underpayment is attributable to a gross valuation misstatement, on the lower thresholds that subsection defines.

Read that against what an appraisal of a closely held interest actually is — an opinion on which competent professionals differ by wide margins — and the planning implication is uncomfortable. The penalty is not keyed to bad faith or to an indefensible method. It is keyed to the ratio between what was reported and what is finally determined, which means an honest appraisal prepared by a credentialed professional can still land inside the provision if the examination moves the number far enough.

Two responses follow, and both are structural rather than argumentative. The first is the defense in [I.R.C. § 6664](https://jaymcdaniel.com/authorities/irc-6664/)(c), which excuses the penalty on a showing of reasonable cause and good faith — and the substance of that showing is the contemporaneous, independent appraisal the taxpayer actually relied on. The second is to build the transfer so that a revaluation reallocates rather than taxes, which is what a defined-value clause does; see *Estate of Petter v. Commissioner*, 653 F.3d 1012 (9th Cir. 2011). The same file that supports the number defends against the penalty for missing it, which is why an appraisal obtained after the notice of examination is worth so much less than one dated before the gift.
