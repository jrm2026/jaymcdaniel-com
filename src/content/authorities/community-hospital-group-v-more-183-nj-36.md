*More* is where the third prong of the *Solari* test — that a covenant not be injurious to the public — stops being decorative and decides a case.

Dr. Jay More joined the New Jersey Neuroscience Institute at John F. Kennedy Medical Center in 1994. Each of his three successive employment agreements carried a post-employment covenant barring him for two years from practising within a thirty-mile radius of the hospital; the later agreements broadened the bar from neurosurgery to the practice of medicine generally. When the relationship ended, the hospital sought to enforce it. The trial court refused a preliminary injunction, the Appellate Division reversed and ordered temporary relief, and the Supreme Court granted leave to reconsider *Karlin v. Weinberg*, 77 N.J. 408 (1978), which had held physician covenants not automatically void.

The Court, per Justice Wallace, declined the invitation to overrule *Karlin*. A restrictive covenant between a hospital and a physician is not per se unreasonable and unenforceable. But it held that on these facts the geographic restriction was excessive and had to be reduced to avoid being detrimental to the public interest. A companion decision issued the same day, *Pierson v. Medical Health Centers, P.A.*, 183 N.J. 65 (2005), addressed the same question between physicians.

The lesson generalises past medicine. New Jersey courts do not choose between enforcing a covenant as written and striking it; under *Solari* they reduce it to what is reasonable, and the public-interest prong is a live constraint on how far the restraint may reach — sharpest where the departing person supplies a service the public needs and the community's access to it is at stake. For anyone valuing a business on the assumption that its covenants will hold, *More* is the reminder that the enforceable covenant may be materially smaller than the drafted one, and that the difference belongs in the valuation rather than in the optimism of the party relying on it.
