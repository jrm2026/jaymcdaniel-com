The corporation restructured to restrict future public sales of its stock, which triggered dissenters' rights, and then sought to buy the dissenters out at a discount for lack of marketability. The Court held the discount inapplicable absent extraordinary circumstances. Applying it would enrich the majority, penalize the minority for invoking the very statute meant to protect them, and encourage squeeze-outs — a set of incentives the appraisal remedy exists to prevent.

The more useful half of the opinion is the part fewer people cite. The valuation date was December 5, 1991. Four and a half years later Alusuisse-Lonza acquired the company at $63 per share. The trial court refused to reopen the record, reasoning that the price was neither known nor knowable on the valuation date. The Supreme Court disagreed and remanded for the limited purpose of considering how that merger price bears on what the corporation was worth at the restructuring.

That is the New Jersey answer to the known-or-knowable objection, and it is worth more than most practitioners realize. The acquisition price did not become fair value. It became evidence against which the court's number could be tested — evidence generated by a strategic acquirer, years after the fact, admitted anyway. A party arguing that real market interest is irrelevant to fair value in New Jersey is arguing against its own Supreme Court.
