DeNike and Cupo were the two equal members of Classic Mortgage, a mortgage-brokering LLC. When the relationship failed, DeNike bought Cupo out, and the parties could not agree on what the interest was worth. The trial court rejected both sides' experts and appointed its own, who valued the company on the express premise that the object was its worth to the surviving member, not to an open market — and therefore applied no discounts. The Appellate Division endorsed the logic: the departure of one producer-member does not make the company more attractive to an outside investor, and the person actually benefited by the buyout is the remaining owner, who takes complete control.

That is the fair-value principle of *Cavalier Oil* worked out in an LLC buyout. Where there is no genuine transfer to a market and the buyer is the controlling insider, a marketability or minority discount misprices the transaction — it hands the acquiring member a reduction that reflects a sale that is not occurring. The case is useful precisely because it reaches that result without a controlling statute forcing it, on the court's reading of what the valuation is actually for.

Two cautions before citing it. First, the holding the buyer wanted — that the entire trial had to be redone because the trial judge negotiated post-retirement employment with plaintiff's counsel shortly before entering judgment — was rejected by the Appellate Division but then sustained by the New Jersey Supreme Court, which found an appearance of impropriety and vacated the orders the judge entered, remanding to a different judge (*DeNike v. Cupo*, 196 N.J. 502 (2008)). Cite the Appellate Division opinion for its valuation reasoning with that subsequent history in view. Second, the panel separately remanded because the valuation date had been set incorrectly; the discount reasoning, not the date mechanics, is what makes the case worth keeping.
