A pediatric practice's operating agreement said that if the members failed to agree on a new company valuation for more than two years, the buyout price would be built off the last agreed value, adjusted for changes in net worth. The members signed a Certificate of Agreed Value at $2.4 million on January 1, 2000, and never signed another one. Dr. Namerow announced his retirement in 2016 — the first retirement in the practice's history, and the first occasion to apply a provision nobody had thought about in sixteen years.

He argued that the periodic fair market value appraisals the members had obtained over the years for planning and settlement purposes established a course of conduct that modified the agreement, and that enforcing the stale certificate breached fiduciary duties owed to him. Neither worked. Where a contract is unambiguous the court will not look outside the four corners to determine intent, and parol evidence will not alter plain meaning. The oppression and fiduciary duty claims were dismissed on summary judgment.

This is the case to read before theorizing about statutory fair value, because a functioning buy-sell displaces the statutory default for the transaction it covers. It is a Chancery Division decision and therefore persuasive rather than binding, but it is published and it is cited regularly for the four-corners rule.

The drafting lesson is worth more than the holding. Nobody at signing intended a valuation frozen at the millennium. The clause failed not because it was unfair but because it contained no consequence for the thing the members were most likely to do, which was nothing. Any agreement keyed to a periodically certified value needs a stated fallback for the years when the certificate does not get signed.
