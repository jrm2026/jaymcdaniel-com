*Sun Dial* is the answer to the owner who assumes that because nobody signed anything, everything walks out the door.

The plaintiff made instrument dials and panels. Its competitive advantage lay in a process for achieving unusual accuracy and finish, and the defendants — former employees — knew it because they had learned it on the job. The company argued it was a trade secret; the defendants answered that the individual ingredients and steps were common knowledge in the trade. The Court held that a process may be a trade secret in the aggregate even where its components are separately well known, and found the record persuasive on the point: competitors had tried to duplicate the dials and failed, one prospective acquirer explored buying the company outright to obtain the process and ultimately took a license instead, and another competitor did the same after its own attempts failed.

The holding that matters for a closely held owner comes next. The plaintiff "never entered into any express agreement with the individual defendants restraining their use of its process after they left its employ." That did not defeat relief. It is enough that the employees learned the employer's trade secret in confidence and, in violation of that confidence, used or disclosed it after the employment ended. The wrongful conduct in breaking the confidence is what supports equitable intervention, independent of any contract.

Read alongside *Whitmyer Bros., Inc. v. Doyle* — which cites *Sun Dial* for exactly this proposition — the practical consequence is that a covenant's real function is evidentiary rather than constitutive. A reasonably confined noncompetitive agreement mainly avoids difficulties of definition and proof; it does not create the protection from nothing. When pricing the risk that a departing owner competes, the analysis should start from what is already protected without paper — trade secrets, confidential business information, and the duties owed while the person is still inside — and then ask what the covenant adds. The answer is usually real but smaller than the party relying on it assumes, and the same discipline cuts the other way against a buyer's expert who assumes the departing owner is wholly unconstrained.
