Section 2704 is the companion to § 2703 and targets a narrower problem: restrictions on liquidation that exist only because the family put them there and can take them away again.

Subsection (a) treats the lapse of a voting or liquidation right in a family-controlled entity as a transfer, which prevents an owner from suppressing the value of what he retains by letting a right expire. Subsection (b) is the one that reaches discounts directly. On a transfer of an interest in a corporation or partnership to or for the benefit of a family member, where the transferor and the transferor's family control the entity immediately before the transfer, any applicable restriction is disregarded in determining the value of the transferred interest.

The definition does the work. An applicable restriction is one that effectively limits the ability of the entity to liquidate and that either lapses in whole or in part after the transfer, or that the transferor or a member of the transferor's family has the right, alone or collectively, to remove after the transfer. Two categories are carved out: a commercially reasonable restriction arising as part of financing with a person unrelated to the transferor or transferee, and any restriction imposed, or required to be imposed, by federal or state law.

For a closely held owner the reading is that a restriction is only worth a discount if somebody outside the family can hold the family to it. A liquidation limit the family wrote and the family can waive is not a real constraint on value; a covenant a lender imposed, or a limitation the state's LLC act supplies, is. The distinction is worth attending to when drafting, because the same clause can be either depending on who controls its removal — and it is worth attending to in the appraisal, because a discount resting on a restriction § 2704(b) disregards has no support left once the statute is applied.
