*Levy* supplied the method *Dugan* adopted five years later, and it is worth reading on its own because it names what the arithmetic is actually measuring. It is an excess-earnings method, not a market method. Determine what a practitioner of comparable experience, expertise, education, and age would earn as an employee in the same locale; average the practitioner's own net income before taxes over a period of years; and where the actual average exceeds the sum of that employee norm and a fair return on physical assets, the excess is the basis for valuing goodwill, capitalized over the number of years a purchaser would pay for in advance.

The framing is the durable part. What the excess-earnings figure captures, *Levy* said, is "the capacity of repeat patronage and of a certain immunity to competition to produce earnings beyond the average for that kind of business." That is enterprise value stated in earnings terms: whatever survives after the practitioner is paid the market rate for his own labor is, by construction, value the practice produces independent of him.

It is a Chancery Division decision, and therefore persuasive rather than binding. But its method is the one every New Jersey professional-goodwill appraisal still runs, and its definition is the reason the reasonable-compensation deduction — not a personal-versus-enterprise label — is where these cases are actually won and lost.
