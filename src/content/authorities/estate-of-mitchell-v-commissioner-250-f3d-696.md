Paul Mitchell, co-founder of the hair-care company John Paul Mitchell Systems, died in 1989. The estate valued its 49.04% block of stock at roughly $28.5 million; the Commissioner asserted a value of $105 million and a deficiency to match. Valuing the stock, the Tax Court began from a $150 million enterprise value and reduced it by ten percent — a key-person discount — to reflect the loss of Mitchell's public presence and creativity, then applied a combined 35% minority and marketability discount at the shareholder level and a further adjustment for pending litigation. *Estate of Mitchell v. Commissioner*, 74 T.C.M. (CCH) 872 (1997). The case is the standard illustration that a key-person discount is a going-concern adjustment taken against enterprise value *before* the interest-level discounts, and that its magnitude is a question of proof rather than label.

The Ninth Circuit vacated and remanded, but not because the key-person discount was improper. It held that the Tax Court erred by failing to shift the burden of proof to the Commissioner once the estate showed that the deficiency notice rested on a mistaken controlling-interest premise, and by failing to explain the methodology behind its valuation — including how it arrived at the specific figures it chose. The lesson is not that key-person discounts are suspect. It is that a number a court cannot trace to a stated method is as vulnerable on review as one left out altogether.

For a closely held owner, *Mitchell* frames both sides of the key-person question. It confirms that the death or departure of a founder whose reputation drives the enterprise is a real, quantifiable haircut to value in a fair-market-value setting such as estate and gift tax. And it warns that the discount lives or dies on the record: a percentage asserted without support fails, and so does a valuation that reaches a result without showing its work. The same discipline governs the mirror-image reasonable-compensation analysis New Jersey courts use to separate enterprise value from an owner's personal goodwill in *Dugan v. Dugan* and *Slutsky v. Slutsky* — measure the dependence, do not assert it.
