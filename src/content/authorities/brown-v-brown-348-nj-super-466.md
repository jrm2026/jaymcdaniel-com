*Brown* takes the discount rule the Supreme Court built for oppressed-shareholder buyouts and moves it into the matrimonial courtroom. James Brown held a 47½% interest in his family's wholesale florist business. Valuing that interest for equitable distribution, the trial court adopted a figure that excluded the marketability and minority discounts his expert had applied, and the Appellate Division affirmed the approach — holding that under the rationale of *Balsamides v. Protameen Chemicals* and *Lawson Mardon Wheaton v. Smith*, and the American Law Institute's Principles of Corporate Governance § 7.22(a), neither discount applies to a closely held interest being valued for equitable distribution.

The reasoning is the same one that governs a fair value buyout, arriving through a different door. In equitable distribution the interest is not sold; it is counted and offset against other marital assets. There is no buyer, no transfer, and no market transaction — so a discount for the difficulty of selling something no one is selling prices an event that is not happening. That is why a matrimonial valuation of a company and a hypothetical-willing-buyer fair market value of the same company can properly diverge, and why importing an estate-and-gift FMV template into a divorce concedes value the standard does not require.

The practical lesson for anyone valuing a closely held interest in a New Jersey divorce is to expect fair-value treatment on discounts and to plead it. Do not accept marketability or minority reductions as a default because the accountant's report carried them; *Brown* is the authority that they do not belong in the equitable-distribution number absent something extraordinary. The Appellate Division remanded on the equitable-distribution, counsel-fee, and expert-fee questions, but the discount holding is the part that travels.
