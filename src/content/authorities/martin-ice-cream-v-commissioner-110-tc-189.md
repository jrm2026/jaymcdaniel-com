*Martin Ice Cream* is the case that separates what a company owns from what its founder merely lends it, and it is decided on a document that did not exist rather than on a valuation. Arnold Strassberg held 51 percent of Martin Ice Cream Company, a New Jersey corporation in Bloomfield. He had built the wholesale distribution of super-premium ice cream into supermarkets on relationships with supermarket owners that predated the company by years, and on a personal handshake understanding with Reuben Mattus, the founder of Häagen-Dazs. When Häagen-Dazs paid $1,430,340 for the supermarket distribution rights, the Commissioner argued that the corporation was the true seller and owed corporate-level tax on the gain.

The Tax Court held that the corporation had never owned the assets sold. Strassberg "never entered into a covenant not to compete with petitioner or any other agreement — not even an employment agreement — by which any of [his] distribution agreements ..., relationships with the supermarkets, and ... distribution expertise became the property of petitioner." Drawing on *Estate of Taracido v. Commissioner*, 72 T.C. 1014 (1979), *Cullen v. Commissioner*, 14 T.C. 368 (1950), and *MacDonald v. Commissioner*, 3 T.C. 720 (1944), the court restated a long-standing rule: personal relationships of a shareholder-employee are not corporate assets when the employee has no employment contract with the corporation, and they are "entirely distinct from the intangible corporate asset of corporate goodwill." What the corporation did not own, it could not transfer.

For an owner of a closely held business the holding runs well past its tax setting. It supplies the strongest version of the personal-goodwill argument, and that argument is categorically different from a key-person discount. A key-person discount reduces the value of an asset the entity owns and invites the reply that the person is not actually leaving. *Martin Ice Cream* says the value was never the entity's at all — which removes an asset from the balance sheet rather than shading a percentage. New Jersey reaches the same line from the matrimonial side in *Dugan v. Dugan*, where the distributable portion of goodwill is the portion that survives independent of the individual.

Read as planning rather than as controversy, the case is a warning. The corporation had operated for a decade and a half on assets it did not own, and it had no way to sell them. The covenant that would have fixed this costs almost nothing at formation and cannot be bought once someone is contemplating departure. Note also that the taxpayer did not win outright: the court sustained gain on the redemption of Strassberg's stock, though far less than the Commissioner determined, and upheld the substantial-understatement addition to tax.
