Twenty years and three appeals into an oppressed-shareholder fight among the Walsh siblings, the only questions left were about a number: whether the valuation the trial court had already adopted secretly contained a marketability discount, and, if it did not, what discount to apply. On the prior remand the Appellate Division had directed the trial judge to answer exactly those two questions. He found, after another round of expert testimony, that no marketability discount had yet been embedded, and he applied one of twenty-five percent. Norbert Walsh appealed both the finding and the figure; his siblings cross-appealed for a larger discount. The panel affirmed.

The case earns its place not for a new rule but for what it shows about how a fair-value number survives appeal. The trial judge did not adopt any expert's discount wholesale. He fixed twenty-five percent — a figure no witness had specifically endorsed — and the Appellate Division held he was entitled to, "so long as it was plausible, based on evidence in the record, and — in the final analysis — fair and equitable," quoting *City of Long Branch v. Liu*, 203 N.J. 464 (2010). That is the standard a practitioner is really litigating against in a New Jersey oppression buyout: not whether the court will adopt your expert's discount, but whether the court's own figure has record support and reads as equitable. Once it does, neither side gets to second-guess a trial judge's thoughtful and well-reasoned determination.

Read it as the enforcement half of *Balsamides* and *Lawson Mardon Wheaton*. Those cases decide when a marketability discount applies; *Wisniewski* shows how much deference the resulting number draws once a trial court has done the work. It is unpublished, and therefore persuasive rather than binding under *R.* 1:36-3 — cite it for the appellate posture and the *Liu* standard, not as controlling authority.
