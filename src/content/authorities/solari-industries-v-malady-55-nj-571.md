*Solari* is the foundation of New Jersey restrictive covenant law, and it does two things that matter to anyone pricing a departing owner's freedom to compete. Joseph Malady was President, Chief Executive Officer, and a director of the American arm of an Italian manufacturer of airport and railway information boards. His employment contract barred him for one year after termination from selling competing products or diverting business, particularly from customers he had dealt with. It named no geographic area. When Malady left and took a competing franchise, the Chancery Division held the provision void per se for that omission and refused an injunction.

The Supreme Court, per Justice Jacobs, rejected the all-or-nothing rule. A noncompetitive agreement may receive total or partial enforcement to the extent reasonable under the circumstances. The Court expressly declined the mechanical "blue pencil test," under which a covenant survives only if the offending words can be struck and leave something grammatical behind, in favor of the substantive question: whether partial enforcement is possible without injury to the public and without injustice to the parties. Overbreadth is therefore a reason for a court to trim a covenant, not to void it.

The second holding is the one practitioners most often miss, and it is worth real money in a buyout. The Court distinguished two covenants that are usually discussed as though they were one. A seller's noncompetitive covenant, "designed to protect the good will of the business for the buyer," is freely enforceable. An employee's covenant not to compete after termination is not as freely enforceable, "because of well recognized countervailing policy considerations," and is measured against three requirements: that it simply protect the legitimate interests of the employer, impose no undue hardship on the employee, and not be injurious to the public. 55 N.J. at 576.

The practical consequence is a sequencing rule. A covenant signed at a buyout closing, in consideration of the purchase price and as part of the sale of the departing owner's interest and the goodwill attached to it, is a seller's covenant — the enforceable kind. A covenant a controlling owner attempts to impose on a co-owner-employee mid-relationship, for no fresh consideration, is the other kind, and *Whitmyer Bros., Inc. v. Doyle* narrowed what it may protect a year later. The moment of maximum leverage to obtain the covenant is also the moment it is most enforceable. On the facts, the Court remanded for final hearing and suggested that the employer might equitably be awarded a limited restraint against Malady dealing for a year with Solari's actual or prospective customers with whom he had substantial dealings while employed — a useful template for what a durable restraint looks like.
